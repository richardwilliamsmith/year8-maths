# 7B: Circumference and Area of Circles and Sectors (Chapter 7 — Length, Area, Volume and Time)

**VCAA Version 2.0 Alignment**: `VC2M8M02` (Measurement)
> Demonstrate how the formulas for the circumference and area of a circle are established, and use them to solve practical problems.

**Target Mastery**: Calculate exact (in terms of $\pi$) and rounded values for the circumference and area of circles, semicircles, quadrants, and sectors, and remember to include straight radii in sector perimeters.

**Prerequisite Skills**:
- Irrational number $\pi$ (Section 2C)
- Squaring numbers and rounding decimals

### Key Vocabulary
- **Circumference ($C$)**: The total distance (perimeter) around the curved boundary of a circle, given by $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$.
- **Sector**: A 'pizza-slice' region of a circle bounded by two radii and the arc between them, subtending a central angle $\theta$ at the centre.
- **Annulus**: A flat ring-shaped region bounded by two concentric circles of outer radius $R$ and inner radius $r$, with area $A = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(R^2 - r^2)$.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Establishing Circumference ($C = 2\pi r$) and Area ($A = \pi r^2$) of a Circle

For every circle, the ratio of its circumference $C$ to its diameter $d = 2r$ is the constant $\pi \approx 3.14159\dots$, giving $C = \pi d = 2\pi r$. If a circle of radius $r$ is sliced into many narrow sectors and rearranged into an approximate parallelogram, the base length is half the circumference ($\frac{1}{2} \times 2\pi r = \pi r$) and the height is the radius $r$, giving the exact area formula $A = (\pi r) \times r = \pi r^2$.

$$C = \pi d = 2\pi r, \qquad A = \pi r^2$$

> **💡 Area Has $r^2$ (Square Units); Circumference Has $2r$ (Linear Units)**
> Never confuse $2\pi r$ with $\pi r^2$: Area is measured in square units, so it must contain $r^2$.

### Arc Length, Perimeter, and Area of Sectors

A sector with central angle $\theta^\circ$ is the fraction $\frac{\theta}{360}$ of a full circle. A semicircle ($\theta = 180^\circ$) is $\frac{1}{2}$ of a circle, and a quadrant ($\theta = 90^\circ$) is $\frac{1}{4}$ of a circle. While the curved arc length is $l = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r$, the total perimeter of a sector also includes the two straight radius edges ($+ 2r$, or $+ d$ for a semicircle).

$$A_{\text{sector}} = \frac{\theta}{360}\pi r^2, \qquad P_{\text{sector}} = \frac{\theta}{360} \times 2\pi r + 2r$$

> **💡 Don't Forget the Straight Edges in Sector Perimeter**
> The perimeter of a sector or semicircle is a closed boundary: add the curved arc length AND the two straight radii ($+ 2r$).

### Exact Values in Terms of $\pi$ vs Rounded Approximations

When a question asks for an exact answer, leave $\pi$ as a symbol (e.g., a circle of radius $6\text{ cm}$ has exact circumference $12\pi\text{ cm}$ and exact area $36\pi\text{ cm}^2$). Only convert to a decimal at the very final step when rounding is requested.

$$r = 6\text{ cm} \implies A_{\text{exact}} = 36\pi\text{ cm}^2 \approx 113.10\text{ cm}^2 \text{ (2 d.p.)}$$

> **💡 Halve the Diameter Before Using $A = \pi r^2$**
> Always check whether the diagram gives the diameter $d$ or the radius $r$. If given $d$, halve it first: $r = \frac{d}{2}$.

## Worked Examples

### WE7_2_1: Exact and Rounded Circumference and Area of a Circle

**Problem**: A circular centre-bounce circle on an AFL ground has a diameter of $10\text{ m}$. Find its circumference and area: (a) as exact values in terms of $\pi$, and (b) rounded to $2$ decimal places.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | First halve the diameter $d = 10\text{ m}$ to get the radius $r = 5\text{ m}$. Substitute $r = 5$ into $C = 2\pi r$ and $A = \pi r^2$ for exact values. | $$r = \frac{10}{2} = 5\text{ m}$$
$$C = 2\pi(5) = 10\pi\text{ m}, \qquad A = \pi(5)^2 = 25\pi\text{ m}^2$$ |
| 2 | Multiply by $\pi \approx 3.14159\dots$ and round each result to $2$ decimal places. | $$C = 10\pi \approx 31.42\text{ m}, \qquad A = 25\pi \approx 78.54\text{ m}^2$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $C = 10\pi\text{ m}$, $A = 25\pi\text{ m}^2$; (b) $C \approx 31.42\text{ m}$, $A \approx 78.54\text{ m}^2$

> **✏️ Now You Try**

> *Hint*: Radius $r = 8\text{ cm}$, so $C = 2\pi(8)$ and $A = \pi(8)^2$.
> **Working**: $C = 16\pi \approx 50.27\text{ cm}$; $A = 64\pi \approx 201.06\text{ cm}^2$.
> **Answer**: $C = 16\pi \approx 50.27\text{ cm}$, $A = 64\pi \approx 201.06\text{ cm}^2$

### WE7_2_2: Perimeter and Area of a Sector

**Problem**: A sector of a circle has radius $r = 12\text{ cm}$ and central angle $\theta = 60^\circ$. Find (a) its exact area in terms of $\pi$, and (b) its exact perimeter in terms of $\pi$, plus its perimeter rounded to $2$ decimal places.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Simplify the sector fraction $\frac{60}{360} = \frac{1}{6}$ and multiply by $\pi r^2 = \pi(12)^2 = 144\pi$. | $$A = \frac{60}{360} \times \pi(12)^2 = \frac{1}{6} \times 144\pi = 24\pi\text{ cm}^2$$ |
| 2 | Find the curved arc length $\frac{1}{6} \times 2\pi(12) = 4\pi\text{ cm}$, then add the two straight radius edges $2r = 24\text{ cm}$. | $$P = \frac{1}{6} \times 2\pi(12) + 2(12) = (4\pi + 24)\text{ cm} \approx 36.57\text{ cm}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $24\pi\text{ cm}^2$, (b) $(4\pi + 24)\text{ cm} \approx 36.57\text{ cm}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: A quadrant ($\theta = 90^\circ$) has radius $r = 8\text{ cm}$. Find (a) its exact area and (b) its exact perimeter.
> *Hint*: Fraction is $\frac{90}{360} = \frac{1}{4}$. Don't forget $+ 2(8) = 16\text{ cm}$ in the perimeter.
> **Working**: (a) $\frac{1}{4}\pi(8)^2 = 16\pi\text{ cm}^2$; (b) $\frac{1}{4}(2\pi \times 8) + 16 = (4\pi + 16)\text{ cm}$.
> **Answer**: (a) $16\pi\text{ cm}^2$, (b) $(4\pi + 16)\text{ cm}$

### WE7_2_3: Perimeter and Area of an Athletics Track (Stadium Shape)

**Problem**: A community running track consists of a rectangle $80\text{ m}$ long and $40\text{ m}$ wide with a semicircle of diameter $40\text{ m}$ attached to each of the two $40\text{ m}$ ends. Find (a) the exact and rounded perimeter of the track, and (b) the exact and rounded total area enclosed by the track ($2$ d.p.).

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | The two semicircular ends together form one complete circle of diameter $d = 40\text{ m}$ (radius $r = 20\text{ m}$). The outer boundary consists of the two straight $80\text{ m}$ sides plus the circumference of one full circle ($\pi d = 40\pi$). | $$P = 2(80) + 40\pi = (160 + 40\pi)\text{ m} \approx 285.66\text{ m}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $(160 + 40\pi)\text{ m} \approx 285.66\text{ m}$, (b) $(3200 + 400\pi)\text{ m}^2 \approx 4456.64\text{ m}^2$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Substituting the Diameter Instead of the Radius into $A = \pi r^2$
- **The Misconception**: Using $d$ directly in $A = \pi r^2$, making the calculated area $4$ times too large.
- **The Correct Concept**: Because $(2r)^2 = 4r^2$, substituting the diameter instead of the radius multiplies the true area by $4$. Always halve the diameter first.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$d = 10\text{ cm} \implies A = \pi(10)^2 = 100\pi\text{ cm}^2 \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$r = \frac{10}{2} = 5\text{ cm} \implies A = \pi(5)^2 = 25\pi\text{ cm}^2$$
- **Counter-Example**: A circle of diameter $10\text{ cm}$ fits inside a $10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$ square of area $100\text{ cm}^2$, so its area cannot possibly be $100\pi \approx 314.16\text{ cm}^2$.

### Omitting the Straight Diameter When Finding the Perimeter of a Semicircle
- **The Misconception**: Calculating the perimeter of a semicircle of radius $r$ as just the curved arc $\pi r$.
- **The Correct Concept**: A semicircle is a closed shape bounded by a curved arc of length $\pi r$ AND a straight diameter of length $2r$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$P_{\text{semicircle}} = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi r) = \pi r \quad \text{(Incorrect — arc length only)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$P_{\text{semicircle}} = \pi r + 2r = \pi r + d$$
- **Counter-Example**: For $r = 7\text{ cm}$, walking all the way around the semicircle edge and back to the start requires walking the curved arc ($7\pi\text{ cm}$) plus the straight base ($14\text{ cm}$).

### Fluency & Understanding

#### Question `7_2_Q1` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
Find the exact circumference and exact area of a circle of radius $9\text{ cm}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Substitute $r = 9$ into $C = 2\pi r$: $C = 2\pi(9) = 18\pi\text{ cm}$.
2. Substitute $r = 9$ into $A = \pi r^2$: $A = \pi(9)^2 = 81\pi\text{ cm}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $C = 18\pi\text{ cm}$, $A = 81\pi\text{ cm}^2$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q2` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
Using the approximation $\pi \approx \dfrac{22}{7}$, find the circumference and area of a circle of radius $14\text{ cm}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Circumference: $C = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14 = 2 \times 22 \times 2 = 88\text{ cm}$.
2. Area: $A = \frac{22}{7} \times 14^2 = \frac{22}{7} \times 196 = 22 \times 28 = 616\text{ cm}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $C = 88\text{ cm}$, $A = 616\text{ cm}^2$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q3` [Medium] *(Fluency)*
Find the exact perimeter and exact area of a semicircle with diameter $12\text{ m}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The radius is $r = \frac{12}{2} = 6\text{ m}$. The curved arc is $\pi r = 6\pi\text{ m}$, so total perimeter $P = (6\pi + 12)\text{ m}$.
2. The area is half a full circle: $A = \frac{1}{2}\pi(6)^2 = 18\pi\text{ m}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $P = (6\pi + 12)\text{ m}$, $A = 18\pi\text{ m}^2$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q4` [Medium] *(Fluency)*
Calculate the exact area and exact arc length of a sector with radius $15\text{ cm}$ and central angle $120^\circ$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The sector fraction is $\frac{120}{360} = \frac{1}{3}$. Area $= \frac{1}{3} \times \pi(15)^2 = \frac{225\pi}{3} = 75\pi\text{ cm}^2$.
2. Arc length $= \frac{1}{3} \times 2\pi(15) = \frac{30\pi}{3} = 10\pi\text{ cm}$.

**Final Answer**: Area $= 75\pi\text{ cm}^2$, arc length $= 10\pi\text{ cm}$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q5` [Medium] *(Understanding)*
A circle has an exact area of $196\pi\text{ cm}^2$. Find (a) its radius $r$ and (b) its exact circumference.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Equate $\pi r^2 = 196\pi \implies r^2 = 196 \implies r = \sqrt{196} = 14\text{ cm}$.
2. Substitute $r = 14$ into $C = 2\pi r$: $C = 28\pi\text{ cm}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $r = 14\text{ cm}$, (b) $C = 28\pi\text{ cm}$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q6` [Challenging] *(Understanding)*
An annulus (flat washer) is formed by removing an inner circle of radius $5\text{ cm}$ from a concentric outer circle of radius $13\text{ cm}$. Find the exact area of the annulus in terms of $\pi$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Use the annulus area formula $A = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2 = \pi(13^2 - 5^2)$.
2. Evaluate: $A = \pi(169 - 25) = 144\pi\text{ cm}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $144\pi\text{ cm}^2$
</details>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `7_2_Q7` [Medium] *(Cycling & Distance)*
A mountain bike wheel has a diameter of $70\text{ cm}$ ($0.7\text{ m}$). Using $\pi \approx \dfrac{22}{7}$, find (a) the distance travelled in one complete wheel revolution, and (b) how many revolutions the wheel makes during a $4.4\text{ km}$ trail ride in the You Yangs.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. One revolution equals the circumference: $C = \pi d = \frac{22}{7} \times 0.7 = 2.2\text{ m}$.
2. Convert $4.4\text{ km}$ to $4400\text{ m}$ and divide by $2.2\text{ m}$: $\frac{4400}{2.2} = 2000\text{ revolutions}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $2.2\text{ m}$, (b) $2000\text{ revolutions}$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q8` [Challenging] *(Australian Irrigation Farming)*
A centre-pivot irrigation boom on a Murray River lucerne farm rotates in a full circle of radius $200\text{ m}$ inside a square paddock of side length $400\text{ m}$. Find the exact area (and the area rounded to the nearest whole $\text{m}^2$, using $\pi \approx 3.1416$) of the four dry corners of the square paddock that the sprinkler does not reach.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Area of the square paddock $= 400 \times 400 = 160\,000\text{ m}^2$, and area of the irrigated circle $= \pi(200)^2 = 40\,000\pi\text{ m}^2$.
2. Subtract the circle from the square: $A_{\text{dry}} = 160\,000 - 40\,000\pi \approx 160\,000 - 40\,000(3.1416) = 34\,336\text{ m}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $(160\,000 - 40\,000\pi)\text{ m}^2 \approx 34\,336\text{ m}^2$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q9` [Medium] *(Civil Engineering)*
A circular roundabout in Canberra has a radius of $10\text{ m}$ and is surrounded by a $2\text{ m}$ wide asphalt lane (forming an outer radius of $12\text{ m}$). Find the exact area of the asphalt lane in terms of $\pi$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The asphalt lane is an annulus with outer radius $R = 10 + 2 = 12\text{ m}$ and inner radius $r = 10\text{ m}$.
2. Calculate the difference of areas: $A = \pi(12^2 - 10^2) = \pi(144 - 100) = 44\pi\text{ m}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $44\pi\text{ m}^2$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `7_2_Q10` [Medium] *(Scaling Proof)*
Prove algebraically that if the radius of a circle is doubled (from $r$ to $2r$), its circumference doubles ($2 \times$), whereas its area quadruples ($4 \times$).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Original circumference is $C = 2\pi r$ and original area is $A = \pi r^2$.
2. Replacing $r$ with $2r$ gives $C_{\text{new}} = 2\pi(2r) = 2(2\pi r) = 2C$ and $A_{\text{new}} = \pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2 = 4A$.

**Final Answer**: $C_{\text{new}} = 2\pi(2r) = 2(2\pi r) = 2C$; $A_{\text{new}} = \pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2 = 4A$
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q11` [Challenging] *(Consumer Reasoning)*
A pizzeria sells one large pizza of diameter $40\text{ cm}$ ($r = 20\text{ cm}$) for the same price as two medium pizzas of diameter $28\text{ cm}$ ($r = 14\text{ cm}$). Compare the exact total areas in terms of $\pi$ to determine which option gives more pizza.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Area of one large pizza ($r = 20\text{ cm}$): $A_{\text{large}} = \pi(20)^2 = 400\pi\text{ cm}^2$.
2. Combined area of two medium pizzas ($r = 14\text{ cm}$): $2 \times \pi(14)^2 = 2 \times 196\pi = 392\pi\text{ cm}^2$. Since $400\pi > 392\pi$, the single large pizza gives $8\pi\text{ cm}^2$ more pizza.

**Final Answer**: One large pizza gives more ($400\pi\text{ cm}^2$ vs $2 \times 196\pi = 392\pi\text{ cm}^2$)
</details>

#### Question `7_2_Q12` [Extension] *(Dimensional Analysis)*
Find the unique positive value of the radius $r$ for which the numerical value of a circle's area ($\pi r^2$) equals the numerical value of its circumference ($2\pi r$). Explain why the two quantities are still physically different even at this value of $r$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Equate the two formulas for $r > 0$: $\pi r^2 = 2\pi r \implies r = 2$ (where both equal $4\pi$).
2. Even when $r = 2$, circumference measures 1D boundary length (e.g., $4\pi\text{ cm}$) and area measures 2D surface coverage (e.g., $4\pi\text{ cm}^2$), so their units are different.

**Final Answer**: $r = 2$ (numerical values both equal $4\pi$, but circumference is a 1D length in $\text{units}$ while area is a 2D surface in $\text{units}^2$)
</details>
