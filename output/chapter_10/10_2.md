### Key Vocabulary
- **Simple Random, Systematic, and Stratified Sampling**: In simple random sampling, every member has an equal chance of selection (e.g. random number generator). In systematic sampling, every $k$th member is chosen from a list. In stratified sampling, the population is divided into subgroups (strata, such as Year levels) and each group is sampled in exact proportion to its size.
- **Sampling Variability**: The natural variation in summary statistics (such as sample means, medians, or proportions) observed when different random samples are drawn from the exact same population.
- **Distribution Shape (Symmetric, Skewed, Bimodal)**: A distribution is **symmetric** if its left and right halves mirror each other; **positively skewed** (right-skewed) if its long tail stretches towards high values; **negatively skewed** (left-skewed) if its long tail stretches towards low values; and **bimodal** if it has two distinct peaks.
- **Quartiles ($Q_1, Q_3$) and Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR}$)**: The lower quartile $Q_1$ is the median of the lower half of an ordered data set ($25$th percentile) and the upper quartile $Q_3$ is the median of the upper half ($75$th percentile). The interquartile range $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$ measures the spread of the middle $50\%$ of scores.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Random, Systematic, and Stratified Sampling vs Biased Sampling

When a census of an entire population $N$ is too expensive or time-consuming, we select a **sample** of size $n$. To ensure every subgroup of the population is fairly represented, **stratified random sampling** divides the population into strata (such as year levels or age brackets) and selects a random sample from each stratum proportional to its share of the population. By contrast, **convenience sampling** (asking people nearby) and **self-selected/voluntary sampling** (online phone-in polls) introduce systematic bias.

$$\text{Stratum sample size } n_k = \frac{\text{Stratum population } N_k}{\text{Total population } N} \times n_{\text{total}}$$

> **💡 Match Each Subgroup's Percentage in a Stratified Sample**
> If Year 8 students make up $25\%$ of a secondary school's population, then Year 8 students must also make up $25\%$ of a stratified school survey.

### Estimating Population Totals and Capture-Recapture Sampling

If a representative random sample of size $n$ has sample proportion $\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n}$ with a certain trait, we estimate the number of individuals with that trait in the entire population of size $N$ as $\hat{N}_{\text{trait}} = \hat{p} \times N$. Ecologists also estimate an unknown wildlife population $N$ using **capture-recapture**: catch, tag, and release $M$ animals; later catch a second random sample of $n$ animals and count how many are tagged ($m$). Equating the sample tagged proportion $\frac{m}{n}$ to the population tagged proportion $\frac{M}{N}$ gives $N \approx \frac{M \times n}{m}$.

$$\frac{m}{n} \approx \frac{M}{N} \implies \text{Estimated Population } N \approx \frac{M \times n}{m}$$

> **💡 Different Random Samples Give Slightly Different Estimates**
> Because of **sampling variability**, two random samples of size $n = 40$ from the same school will rarely give the exact same sample mean or proportion — averaging across multiple random samples or increasing $n$ gives a more reliable estimate.

### Describing the Shape of a Data Distribution

On a histogram, dot plot, or stem-and-leaf plot, the **shape** of a distribution is classified by its symmetry and peaks:
- **Symmetric**: the data is balanced evenly around the centre, so $\text{Mean} \approx \text{Median}$.
- **Positively skewed (skewed to the right)**: most scores cluster on the left with a long tail stretching towards high positive values; the high tail pulls the mean above the median ($\text{Mean} > \text{Median}$).
- **Negatively skewed (skewed to the left)**: most scores cluster on the right with a long tail stretching towards low values; the low tail pulls the mean below the median ($\text{Mean} < \text{Median}$).
- **Bimodal**: the display has two distinct peaks (modes), often indicating two different groups mixed together.

$$\text{Positively skewed: } \text{Mean} > \text{Median}; \qquad \text{Symmetric: } \text{Mean} \approx \text{Median}; \qquad \text{Negatively skewed: } \text{Mean} < \text{Median}$$

> **💡 The Direction of Skew Is the Direction of the Long Tail!**
> Don't look at where the tall peak is — look at where the **long stretched-out tail** points. A tail stretching to the right (high numbers) is **positively skewed**; a tail stretching to the left (low numbers) is **negatively skewed**.

### Comparing Two Distributions Using Centre and Spread (Range and IQR)

When comparing two data distributions (such as back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots or parallel dot plots), always write a two-part conclusion in context:
1. **Compare Centre** (using the **median** or **mean**) to state which group is higher/faster/larger on average.
2. **Compare Spread** (using the **range** $= x_{\max} - x_{\min}$ or the **interquartile range** $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$) to state which group is more consistent (smaller spread) or more variable (larger spread). When data is skewed or contains outliers, always pair the **median** with the **IQR**.

$$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 \quad (\text{spread of the middle } 50\% \text{ of ordered scores})$$

> **💡 Smaller Spread Means Greater Consistency**
> In sport, manufacturing, or weather, a **smaller** range or IQR means the performance is **more consistent** (less variable).

**Problem**: A secondary college in Ballarat has $800$ students in total: $240$ in Year 7, $200$ in Year 8, $180$ in Year 9, and $180$ in Year 10. The Student Representative Council (SRC) selects a **stratified random sample** of $80$ students to survey about canteen menu changes.
(a) How many students should be randomly selected from each year level?
(b) In the sample of $80$ students, $52$ support adding a sushi bar. Estimate how many students across the entire school of $800$ support adding a sushi bar.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Use the stratified sampling formula $n_k = \frac{N_k}{N} \times n_{\text{total}}$. Notice that the sample of $80$ is exactly $\frac{80}{800} = \frac{1}{10}$ ($10\%$) of the total school population. | $$\text{Year 7: } \frac{240}{800} \times 80 = 24, \qquad \text{Year 8: } \frac{200}{800} \times 80 = 20$$
$$\text{Year 9: } \frac{180}{800} \times 80 = 18, \qquad \text{Year 10: } \frac{180}{800} \times 80 = 18 \quad (\text{Check: } 24+20+18+18=80)$$ |
| 2 | Multiply the sample proportion $\hat{p} = \frac{52}{80}$ by the total school population $N = 800$. | $$\text{Estimated school total} = \frac{52}{80} \times 800 = 52 \times 10 = 520\text{ students}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Year 7: $24$, Year 8: $20$, Year 9: $18$, Year 10: $18$; (b) $520$ students

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Naming Skewness After the Tall Peak Instead of the Long Tail
- **The Misconception**: Calling a distribution with a tall peak on the left and a long tail stretching to the right 'negatively/left skewed'.
- **The Correct Concept**: Skewness is named after the direction of the **long tail** (where the extreme scores pull the mean), NOT the location of the peak. A tail stretching to the right (towards higher positive numbers) is **positively skewed** (right-skewed).
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Peak on left, long tail to the right (high values)} \implies \text{Left/negatively skewed} \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Long tail stretches to the right (high positive values)} \implies \text{Positively skewed (right-skewed), with Mean} > \text{Median}$$
- **Counter-Example**: Australian household incomes have most earners clustered on the left and a long tail of multi-millionaires to the right — this is the classic example of a **positively skewed** distribution.

### Interpreting a Larger Range or IQR as 'Better' or 'More Consistent'
- **The Misconception**: Claiming that a player or machine with a larger IQR or range is 'more consistent' because the number is bigger.
- **The Correct Concept**: Range and IQR measure **variability** (how spread out the scores are). A **smaller** range or IQR means the scores are tightly clustered together, which indicates **greater consistency** (reliability).
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Batter A has IQR } = 35 \text{ and Batter B has IQR } = 8 \implies \text{Batter A is more consistent} \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Batter B has the smaller spread } (\text{IQR} = 8 < 35) \implies \text{Batter B is much more consistent}$$
- **Counter-Example**: A batter who scores $40, 42, 41, 39, 43$ has a tiny range of $4$ (extremely consistent), whereas a batter scoring $0, 95, 2, 88, 5$ has a huge range of $95$ (highly inconsistent).

A town council has $12\,000$ adult residents partitioned into three age strata: $18$–$34$ years ($3600$ residents), $35$–$59$ years ($5400$ residents), and $60+$ years ($3000$ residents). How many residents from each age group must be chosen to form a proportional **stratified sample** of $200$ residents?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

#### Question `10_2_Q4` [Medium] *(Understanding)*

#### Question `10_2_Q9` [Challenging] *(Robustness of IQR vs Range)*
Explain why the **Interquartile Range ($\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$)** is a much more reliable measure of spread than the **Range ($x_{\max} - x_{\min}$)** when comparing two skewed distributions or data sets that contain outliers. Illustrate your explanation by finding how much the Range and the IQR of the $7$ scores $10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20$ change if the maximum score $20$ is replaced by an outlier of $95$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. In the original set $10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20$, $Q_1 = 12$ and $Q_3 = 18$, so $\text{IQR} = 18 - 12 = 6$ and $\text{Range} = 20 - 10 = 10$.
2. When $20$ is replaced by $95$, the new Range jumps to $95 - 10 = 85$, whereas $Q_1 = 12$ and $Q_3 = 18$ are unaffected, keeping $\text{IQR} = 6$. Because $\text{IQR}$ trims off the top $25\%$ and bottom $25\%$ of scores, it is resistant to extreme outliers.

**Final Answer**: For $10, 12, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20$, Range $= 10$ and $\text{IQR} = 18 - 12 = 6$. Replacing $20$ with $95$ inflates the Range from $10$ to $85$, while the $\text{IQR}$ remains $18 - 12 = 6$ (completely unchanged) because $\text{IQR}$ depends only on the middle $50\%$ of scores.
</details>

#### Question `10_2_Q10` [Medium] *(Census vs Sample Limitations)*
State two practical scenarios where conducting a **sample** is strictly necessary (not merely cheaper) because a **census** is impossible or destructive.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

#### Question `10_2_Q11` [Challenging] *(Self-Selection Bias vs Sample Size)*
A radio host asks listeners to phone in and vote 'Yes' or 'No' on whether public transport should be free. Out of $2000$ callers, $82\%$ vote 'Yes'. A statistician conducts a random telephone survey of $200$ Victorian voters and finds $54\%$ vote 'Yes'. Which survey is more trustworthy, and why doesn't the radio poll's $10$-times-larger sample size make it more accurate?

<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The radio poll suffers from both **audience bias** (only listeners of that programme hear the question) and **voluntary response bias** (only people passionate enough to call in participate).
2. A smaller **random sample** ($n = 200$) where every voter has an equal chance of selection provides an unbiased estimate, whereas a large biased sample simply repeats the same systematic error $2000$ times.

**Final Answer**: The statistician's random sample of $200$ is far more trustworthy. The radio phone-in is a **self-selected (voluntary response) sample** attracting listeners with strong opinions from one station's audience; increasing the size of a biased sample does not remove selection bias.

#### Question `10_2_Q12` [Extension] *(Critiquing Ecological Mathematical Models)*
State two mathematical assumptions that must hold in the field for the **capture-recapture** formula $N \approx \frac{M \times n}{m}$ to give an accurate estimate of an animal population, and explain how the estimate $N$ is affected if tagged animals become 'trap-shy' (avoiding traps during the second capture).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The model assumes the population is closed, tags remain attached, and tagged individuals mix randomly and have the exact same probability of being caught as untagged individuals.
2. If tagged animals become trap-shy, the recaptured count $m$ is artificially small. Dividing $M \times n$ by a smaller denominator $m$ produces an artificially **large** (overestimated) value for $N$.

**Final Answer**: Assumptions: (1) Closed population (no births, deaths, or migration between captures) and (2) Tags do not fall off or alter the probability of recapture (complete mixing). If tagged animals become trap-shy, fewer tagged animals $m$ are recaptured; since $m$ is in the denominator of $\frac{M \times n}{m}$, a smaller $m$ causes $N$ to be **overestimated**.
</details>
