### Key Vocabulary
- **3D Cartesian Coordinates $(x, y, z)$**: An ordered triple specifying the position of a point in three-dimensional space using three mutually perpendicular axes meeting at the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$: the $x$-axis (length/east-west), the $y$-axis (width/north-south), and the $z$-axis (vertical height/up-down).
- **3D Translation Vector $[a, b, c]$**: A rigid transformation that shifts every point $(x, y, z)$ of a 3D object to its image $(x', y', z') = (x + a, y + b, z + c)$ without rotating or resizing the object.
- **3D Midpoint and Space Diagonal**: Between $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, the midpoint is $M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}, \frac{z_1+z_2}{2}\right)$ and the straight-line distance is $d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2}$.
- **Tessellation Algorithm**: A decision procedure that tests whether polygons can tile a flat plane without gaps or overlaps by checking whether the interior angles meeting at every vertex sum to exactly $360^\circ$.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Locating Points $(x, y, z)$ and Vertices of Prisms in 3D Space

To extend the 2D Cartesian plane into three-dimensional space, we add a vertical $z$-axis perpendicular to both the $x$-axis and $y$-axis at the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$. Every point is uniquely described by an ordered triple $(x, y, z)$. On the $xy$-floor plane, $z = 0$; on the $xz$-wall plane, $y = 0$; and on the $yz$-wall plane, $x = 0$. For an axis-aligned rectangular prism with one corner at $(0, 0, 0)$ and opposite corner at $(L, W, H)$, all $8$ vertices are formed by choosing either $0$ or $L$ for $x$, $0$ or $W$ for $y$, and $0$ or $H$ for $z$.

$$M = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \; \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}, \; \frac{z_1 + z_2}{2}\right), \qquad d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}$$

> **💡 Follow the Axes in Alphabetical Order: $x$, then $y$, then $z$**
> To plot $(4, -3, 5)$ from the origin $(0, 0, 0)$: move $4$ units along the $+x$-axis, then $3$ units in the $-y$ direction on the horizontal floor, and finally $5$ units straight up parallel to the $+z$-axis.

### 3D Coordinate Transformations: Translations and Reflections

A **translation** in 3D space shifts a point $P(x, y, z)$ by adding constants $(a, b, c)$ to each coordinate: $P'(x + a, y + b, z + c)$. Reflections across the coordinate planes reverse the sign of the perpendicular coordinate: reflecting across the horizontal $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) maps $(x, y, z) \to (x, y, -z)$; reflecting across the $yz$-plane ($x = 0$) maps $(x, y, z) \to (-x, y, z)$; and reflecting across the $xz$-plane ($y = 0$) maps $(x, y, z) \to (x, -y, z)$.

$$\text{Translation by } [a, b, c]: \quad (x, y, z) \longrightarrow (x + a, \; y + b, \; z + c)$$

> **💡 Image Minus Object Gives the Translation Vector**
> To find the translation that moves $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ to its image $A'(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, subtract the starting coordinates from the image coordinates: $[a, b, c] = [x_2 - x_1, \; y_2 - y_1, \; z_2 - z_1]$.

### Geometric Classification Algorithms for Triangles and Quadrilaterals

Decision trees and flowcharts can classify geometric figures systematically from their side lengths and angles. For a triangle with ordered side lengths $a \le b \le c$: first check the **Triangle Inequality** ($a + b > c$). If valid, classify **by sides** ($a = b = c \implies$ equilateral; $a = b$ or $b = c \implies$ isosceles; $a < b < c \implies$ scalene) and **by angles** using the converse of Pythagoras' theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \implies$ right-angled; $a^2 + b^2 > c^2 \implies$ acute-angled; $a^2 + b^2 < c^2 \implies$ obtuse-angled).

$$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \;(\text{Right}), \qquad a^2 + b^2 > c^2 \;(\text{Acute}), \qquad a^2 + b^2 < c^2 \;(\text{Obtuse})$$

> **💡 Always Check the Triangle Inequality First!**
> Before classifying a triangle with side lengths $a \le b \le c$, an algorithm must test whether $a + b > c$. For example, $(3, 4, 8)$ cannot form a triangle at all because $3 + 4 = 7 \le 8$.

### Algorithms for Regular Polygon Interior Angles and Tessellations

A regular $n$-gon has interior angle sum $(n - 2) \times 180^\circ$ and each interior angle equals $\theta = \frac{(n - 2) \times 180^\circ}{n} = 180^\circ - \frac{360^\circ}{n}$. An algorithm to test whether a single regular $n$-gon can tessellate (tile the plane edge-to-edge) checks whether $\theta$ divides $360^\circ$ exactly, so that an integer number of polygons $k = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} = \frac{2n}{n - 2}$ can meet around each vertex without gaps or overlaps.

$$\theta = \frac{(n - 2) \times 180^\circ}{n}, \qquad \text{Regular tessellation condition: } k = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} \text{ is an integer}$$

> **💡 Only Three Regular Polygons Tessellate Alone**
> Only $n = 3$ (equilateral triangles, $\theta = 60^\circ$, $k = 6$), $n = 4$ (squares, $\theta = 90^\circ$, $k = 4$), and $n = 6$ (regular hexagons, $\theta = 120^\circ$, $k = 3$) divide $360^\circ$ exactly.

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Swapping the $y$- and $z$-Coordinates When Reading 3D Points
- **The Misconception**: Assuming the second coordinate in $(x, y, z)$ is vertical height because $y$ is vertical on a 2D wall graph.
- **The Correct Concept**: In standard 3D architectural, engineering, and mathematical coordinate systems, $(x, y)$ forms the horizontal floor plane and the third coordinate $z$ represents vertical height (altitude). Always read $(x, y, z)$ in strict alphabetical order.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Point }5\text{ units east } (x), 0\text{ units north } (y), 9\text{ units up } (z) \to (5, 9, 0) \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{East } x = 5, \text{ North } y = 0, \text{ Up } z = 9 \implies (5, 0, 9)$$
- **Counter-Example**: $(5, 9, 0)$ lies flat on the horizontal floor ($z = 0$), whereas $(5, 0, 9)$ is $9$ units above the floor.

### Assuming Every Regular Polygon Can Tessellate on Its Own
- **The Misconception**: Believing that regular pentagons ($n = 5$) or regular octagons ($n = 8$) can tile a flat floor without gaps using only one shape.
- **The Correct Concept**: For a regular $n$-gon to tessellate by itself, its interior angle $\theta = \frac{(n-2)\times 180^\circ}{n}$ must divide $360^\circ$ with zero remainder. A regular pentagon has $\theta = 108^\circ$ ($360 \div 108 = 3.\overline{3}$, not an integer) and a regular octagon has $\theta = 135^\circ$ ($360 \div 135 = 2.\overline{6}$, not an integer).
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Regular octagon } (n = 8): \theta = 135^\circ \implies \text{tiles bathroom floors alone} \quad \text{(Incorrect — requires small squares!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{At a vertex of two octagons: } 2 \times 135^\circ = 270^\circ, \text{ leaving a } 360^\circ - 270^\circ = 90^\circ \text{ square gap}$$
- **Counter-Example**: Three pentagons at a vertex sum to $3 \times 108^\circ = 324^\circ$ (leaving a $36^\circ$ gap), while four pentagons sum to $432^\circ > 360^\circ$ (overlapping).

#### Question `8_3_Q4` [Medium] *(Understanding)*
Use the interior angle formula $\theta = \frac{(n - 2) \times 180^\circ}{n}$ to calculate one interior angle of a regular decagon ($n = 10$), and test whether a regular decagon can tessellate the plane by itself.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

**Final Answer**: $\theta = 144^\circ$; No, because $\frac{360^\circ}{144^\circ} = 2.5$ is not an integer

#### Question `8_3_Q7` [Medium] *(Tiling & Architecture)*
A bathroom tiler creates a **semi-regular tessellation** by placing two regular octagons ($n = 8$) and one regular polygon $P$ around every vertex so that their interior angles sum to $360^\circ$ with no gaps. Use an angle-sum algorithm to find the required interior angle of polygon $P$ and identify what regular polygon $P$ is.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Each interior angle of a regular octagon ($n = 8$) is $\theta_8 = \frac{(8 - 2) \times 180^\circ}{8} = 135^\circ$.
2. Two octagons meeting at a vertex occupy $2 \times 135^\circ = 270^\circ$. The remaining angle needed to complete $360^\circ$ is $360^\circ - 270^\circ = 90^\circ$, which is the interior angle of a square ($n = 4$).

**Final Answer**: Interior angle of $P = 90^\circ$, so $P$ is a square ($n = 4$)
</details>

#### Question `8_3_Q8` [Challenging] *(Warehouse Robotics Optimisation)*
A robot arm in an automated warehouse moves in 3D space from $(1, 3, 2)$ to $(10, 15, 2)$ across the horizontal plane $z = 2$, and then lifts straight up to $(10, 15, 10)$. (a) Find the distance travelled along this two-stage path. (b) If an algorithm reprograms the arm to move in a single straight 3D line from $(1, 3, 2)$ directly to $(10, 15, 10)$, how many metres of travel distance are saved?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Stage 1 horizontal distance $= \sqrt{(10 - 1)^2 + (15 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{81 + 144} = \sqrt{225} = 15\text{ m}$. Stage 2 vertical lift $= 10 - 2 = 8\text{ m}$. Two-stage total $= 15 + 8 = 23\text{ m}$.
2. Direct 3D distance $= \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{225 + 64} = \sqrt{289} = 17\text{ m}$. Distance saved $= 23 - 17 = 6\text{ m}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $23\text{ m}$, (b) Direct distance $= 17\text{ m}$, saving $6\text{ m}$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `8_3_Q9` [Medium] *(Debugging Geometric Classification Algorithms)*
A student writes a quadrilateral classification flowchart with the decision rule: **If** all four sides are equal ($s_1 = s_2 = s_3 = s_4$), **Then** output `'Square'`. Explain why this algorithm is logically flawed, state which geometric shape will be misclassified, and write an additional condition needed to distinguish a square from that shape.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Having four equal sides is the defining property of a **rhombus**. Every square is a rhombus, but a rhombus with angles other than $90^\circ$ is not a square.
2. To fix the algorithm, add a nested decision: after confirming $s_1 = s_2 = s_3 = s_4$, check **If** one interior angle $= 90^\circ$ (or diagonal $d_1 = d_2$), output `'Square'`; **Else**, output `'Rhombus'`.

**Final Answer**: A non-square rhombus also has $4$ equal sides and would be misclassified as a square. The algorithm must also check that at least one interior angle is $90^\circ$ (or that the two diagonals are equal in length).
</details>

#### Question `8_3_Q10` [Medium] *(Algebraic Reasoning in 3D)*
Suppose $M(4, -1, 7)$ is the midpoint of the 3D line segment $AB$, where $A$ has coordinates $(1, 5, 2)$. Design a reverse-midpoint calculation to find the coordinates of endpoint $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $x_M = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}$, rearranging for $x_2$ gives $x_2 = 2x_M - x_1$ (and similarly for $y_2$ and $z_2$).
2. Calculate each coordinate: $x_2 = 2(4) - 1 = 7$, $y_2 = 2(-1) - 5 = -7$, and $z_2 = 2(7) - 2 = 12$, giving $B(7, -7, 12)$.

**Final Answer**: $B(7, -7, 12)$
</details>

#### Question `8_3_Q11` [Challenging] *(Proof in Geometric Algorithms)*
Prove algebraically why no regular polygon with more than $6$ sides ($n > 6$) can ever tessellate the plane by itself.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. At $n = 6$ (a regular hexagon), the interior angle is $\theta = 120^\circ$, so exactly $k = \frac{360^\circ}{120^\circ} = 3$ hexagons meet at a vertex.
2. For any regular polygon with $n > 6$, the interior angle satisfies $120^\circ < \theta < 180^\circ$. Dividing $360^\circ$ by $\theta$ gives $2 < k < 3$. Since there is no whole number between $2$ and $3$, $k$ can never be an integer for $n > 6$.

**Final Answer**: For $n > 6$, the interior angle $\theta = 180^\circ - \frac{360^\circ}{n}$ is strictly between $120^\circ$ and $180^\circ$, so $\frac{360^\circ}{\theta}$ lies strictly between $2$ and $3$, where no integer exists.
</details>

#### Question `8_3_Q12` [Extension] *(3D Vector & Midpoint Proofs)*
Four points in 3D space are $A(1, 2, 3)$, $B(5, 4, 7)$, $C(6, 9, 10)$, and $D(2, 7, 6)$. Use 3D translation vectors (or midpoints of the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$) to prove that $ABCD$ is a parallelogram in 3D space.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The translation vector from $A(1, 2, 3)$ to $B(5, 4, 7)$ is $[5 - 1, 4 - 2, 7 - 3] = [4, 2, 4]$. The translation vector from $D(2, 7, 6)$ to $C(6, 9, 10)$ is $[6 - 2, 9 - 7, 10 - 6] = [4, 2, 4]$.
2. Because opposite sides $AB$ and $DC$ are parallel and equal in length (and the midpoint of both $AC$ and $BD$ is $(3.5, 5.5, 6.5)$), $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.

**Final Answer**: Both $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{DC}$ have the same translation vector $[4, 2, 4]$, and diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ share the exact same midpoint $\left(3.5, 5.5, 6.5\right)$, proving $ABCD$ is a parallelogram.
</details>
