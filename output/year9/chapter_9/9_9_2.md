### Key Vocabulary
- **Relative frequency**: The proportion of trials in which an event $E$ occurs during an experiment or survey of $n$ total trials: $\text{Relative frequency} = \frac{f}{n}$, used as an empirical estimate of $\Pr(E)$.
- **Inclusive 'or' ($A \cup B$) vs exclusive 'or'**: In mathematics, '$A$ or $B$' is inclusive by default ($A$ occurs, or $B$ occurs, or both $A \cap B$ occur); exclusive 'or' means 'either $A$ or $B$, but NOT both' ($n(A \cup B) - n(A \cap B)$).
- **Addition Rule for probability**: For any two events $A$ and $B$, $\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$, where subtracting $\Pr(A \cap B)$ corrects for double-counting the overlap.
- **Monte Carlo simulation**: The use of random physical devices (coins, dice, spinners) or digital random number generators to model a real-world multi-stage chance process over many repeated trials.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Relative Frequency and the Law of Large Numbers

When theoretical probabilities cannot be determined by symmetry (for example, weather patterns, medical recovery rates, or consumer survey responses), we estimate the probability of an event $E$ using its relative frequency $\hat{p} = \frac{f}{n} = \frac{\text{number of times } E \text{ occurred}}{\text{total number of trials } n}$. As the number of trials $n$ increases, the relative frequency fluctuates less and converges towards the true underlying probability. To find the expected number of occurrences in $N$ future trials, calculate $E(X) = N \times \Pr(E)$.

$$\Pr(E) \approx \text{Relative Frequency} = \frac{f}{n}, \qquad \text{Expected frequency} = N \times \Pr(E)$$

> **💡 More Trials Give More Reliable Estimates**
> When comparing two experimental estimates of the same probability, always trust the experiment with the larger sample size $n$ (or pool all trials together by adding the numerators and adding the denominators).

### Compound Events: 'And', Inclusive 'Or', Exclusive 'Or', and the Addition Rule

From a two-way table or Venn diagram of survey frequencies: (1) '$A$ and $B$' is the intersection $A \cap B$; (2) Inclusive '$A$ or $B$' is the union $A \cup B$, which satisfies the Addition Rule $\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$ (if $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0$); (3) Exclusive '$A$ or $B$' means $A$ or $B$ but NOT both, whose probability is $\Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - 2\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A \cup B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$.

$$\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B), \qquad \Pr(A \text{ xor } B) = \Pr(A \cup B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$$

> **💡 Subtract the Overlap Once for Inclusive 'Or', Twice for Exclusive 'Or'**
> Adding $\Pr(A) + \Pr(B)$ counts the intersection $A \cap B$ twice. Subtract $\Pr(A \cap B)$ once to keep the overlap (inclusive 'or'), or subtract $2\Pr(A \cap B)$ to remove the overlap completely (exclusive 'or').

### Conditional Relative Frequency and Designing Simulations (VC2M9P03)

When a question restricts the group using phrases like 'given that', 'of those who...', or 'among the students who...', the denominator changes from the grand total $n$ to the subtotal of that condition: $\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)}$. To simulate a real-world event with probability $p = \frac{a}{b}$, assign $a$ equally likely outcomes of a random generator (such as digits $0\text{--}9$, a die, or random integers $1\text{--}100$) to 'Success' and the remaining $b - a$ outcomes to 'Failure', and repeat for many multi-step trials.

$$\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)} = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(B)}$$

> **💡 'Given That' Shrinks the Denominator**
> Whenever you see 'given that $B$' or 'of the people in row/column $B$', immediately replace the grand total in the denominator with the row/column total $n(B)$.

## Worked Examples

### WE9_9_2_1: Calculating 'And', Inclusive 'Or', and Exclusive 'Or' from Survey Data

**Problem**: A survey of $200$ Year 9 students in Victoria found that $110$ play Australian Rules Football ($F$), $85$ play Basketball ($B$), and $35$ play both sports ($F \cap B$). Estimate using relative frequencies the probability that a randomly chosen student plays: (a) both sports ($F \text{ and } B$), (b) Football or Basketball (inclusive 'or'), (c) Football or Basketball, but not both (exclusive 'or'), and (d) neither sport.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Divide $n(F \cap B) = 35$ by $200$ for part (a), and apply the Addition Rule $n(F \cup B) = n(F) + n(B) - n(F \cap B)$ for part (b). | $$\Pr(F \cap B) = \frac{35}{200} = \frac{7}{40} = 0.175$$
$$n(F \cup B) = 110 + 85 - 35 = 160 \implies \Pr(F \cup B) = \frac{160}{200} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8$$ |
| 2 | For exclusive 'or' in part (c), subtract the overlap $35$ from the union $160$ ($160 - 35 = 125$). For part (d), subtract the union $160$ from $200$. | $$\Pr(F \text{ xor } B) = \frac{160 - 35}{200} = \frac{125}{200} = \frac{5}{8} = 0.625$$
$$\Pr(\text{neither}) = 1 - \frac{4}{5} = \frac{40}{200} = \frac{1}{5} = 0.2$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{7}{40} = 0.175$, (b) $\frac{4}{5} = 0.8$, (c) $\frac{5}{8} = 0.625$, (d) $\frac{1}{5} = 0.2$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: In a survey of $100$ café customers, $62$ ordered coffee ($C$), $48$ ordered a muffin ($M$), and $25$ ordered both. Find the relative frequency of a customer ordering coffee or a muffin (inclusive 'or'), and ordering exclusive 'or' (one but not both).
> *Hint*: Use $n(C \cup M) = 62 + 48 - 25 = 85$, then subtract $25$ more for exclusive 'or'.
> **Working**: Inclusive 'or': $\frac{85}{100} = \frac{17}{20} = 0.85$. Exclusive 'or': $\frac{85 - 25}{100} = \frac{60}{100} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.60$.
> **Answer**: Inclusive 'or': $0.85$ ($\frac{17}{20}$); Exclusive 'or': $0.60$ ($\frac{3}{5}$)

### WE9_9_2_2: Relative Frequency and Conditional Probability in a Two-Way Table

**Problem**: A roadside inspection tested $300$ vehicles in regional Victoria for tyre tread ($T$: Safe vs Worn) and headlights ($H$: Working vs Faulty). Exactly $240$ vehicles had safe tyres (of which $225$ had working headlights and $15$ had faulty headlights), and $60$ vehicles had worn tyres (of which $45$ had working headlights and $15$ had faulty headlights). Find the relative frequency that a vehicle: (a) has worn tyres OR faulty headlights (inclusive), and (b) has faulty headlights GIVEN THAT it has worn tyres.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | From the counts, $n(\text{Worn tyres}) = 60$, $n(\text{Faulty lights}) = 15 + 15 = 30$, and their overlap is $n(\text{Worn} \cap \text{Faulty}) = 15$. Apply the Addition Rule over $300$. | $$\Pr(\text{Worn} \cup \text{Faulty}) = \frac{60 + 30 - 15}{300} = \frac{75}{300} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$$ |
| 2 | For 'given that it has worn tyres', restrict the denominator to the $60$ vehicles with worn tyres, of which $15$ have faulty headlights. | $$\Pr(\text{Faulty} \mid \text{Worn}) = \frac{15}{60} = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$, (b) $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Using the same $300$ vehicles, find the probability that a vehicle has worn tyres GIVEN THAT it has faulty headlights.
> *Hint*: Restrict the denominator to the $30$ vehicles with faulty headlights ($15 + 15 = 30$).
> **Working**: Out of $30$ vehicles with faulty headlights, $15$ have worn tyres: $\Pr(\text{Worn} \mid \text{Faulty}) = \frac{15}{30} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$.
> **Answer**: $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$

### WE9_9_2_3: Analysing a Multi-Step Simulation (Cereal Box Collector's Problem)

**Problem**: A cereal company places $1$ of $2$ collectible AFL footy cards (Card $A$ or Card $B$, equally likely with probability $0.5$ each) inside every box. To estimate how many boxes a fan must buy to collect BOTH cards, a class simulates buying boxes by tossing a coin ($\text{H} = A, \text{T} = B$) until both $\text{H}$ and $\text{T}$ have appeared. Across $50$ simulated trials, the number of boxes required was: $2$ boxes ($26$ times), $3$ boxes ($12$ times), $4$ boxes ($6$ times), $5$ boxes ($4$ times), and $6$ boxes ($2$ times). (a) Based on the simulation, estimate the probability that a fan collects both cards in at most $3$ boxes. (b) Calculate the exact theoretical probability of collecting both cards in at most $3$ boxes and compare.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Add the frequencies for $2$ boxes ($26$) and $3$ boxes ($12$) and divide by $50$ trials. | $$\text{Relative frequency (at most } 3\text{ boxes)} = \frac{26 + 12}{50} = \frac{38}{50} = \frac{19}{25} = 0.76$$ |
| 2 | The only way NOT to have both cards after $3$ boxes is to get $3$ identical cards in a row ($AAA$ or $BBB$), which is $2$ out of $2^3 = 8$ outcomes. | $$\Pr(\text{both cards in } \le 3\text{ boxes}) = 1 - \Pr(AAA \text{ or } BBB) = 1 - \frac{2}{8} = \frac{6}{8} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $0.76$ ($\frac{19}{25}$), (b) Theoretical probability is $0.75$ ($\frac{3}{4}$), which is very close to the simulation estimate of $0.76$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: From the same $50$ simulation trials ($26$ of length $2$, $12$ of length $3$, $6$ of length $4$, $4$ of length $5$, $2$ of length $6$), calculate the experimental mean number of boxes bought per trial.
> *Hint*: Compute $\frac{2(26) + 3(12) + 4(6) + 5(4) + 6(2)}{50}$.
> **Working**: Total boxes $= 52 + 36 + 24 + 20 + 12 = 144$. Mean $= \frac{144}{50} = 2.88$ boxes.
> **Answer**: $2.88$ boxes

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Forgetting to Subtract the Intersection in the Addition Rule
- **The Misconception**: Calculating $\Pr(A \text{ or } B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B)$ when events $A$ and $B$ can occur together (non-mutually exclusive).
- **The Correct Concept**: When $A$ and $B$ overlap, every outcome in $A \cap B$ is counted once in $\Pr(A)$ and a second time in $\Pr(B)$. You must subtract $\Pr(A \cap B)$ so that overlapping outcomes are counted only once.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\Pr(\text{King or Heart}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} = \frac{17}{52} \quad \text{(Incorrect — double-counts King of Hearts!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\Pr(\text{King or Heart}) = \frac{4}{52} + \frac{13}{52} - \frac{1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}$$
- **Counter-Example**: If $\Pr(A) = 0.6$ and $\Pr(B) = 0.6$, simply adding gives $\Pr(A \cup B) = 1.2 > 1$, which is impossible for any probability.

### Using the Grand Total Instead of the Condition Subtotal for 'Given That'
- **The Misconception**: Dividing $n(A \cap B)$ by the grand total $n$ when the question asks for the probability of $A$ GIVEN THAT $B$ occurred.
- **The Correct Concept**: In $\Pr(A \cap B)$ ('A and B'), the denominator is the grand total $n$. In $\Pr(A \mid B)$ ('A given B'), the sample space is restricted exclusively to group $B$, so the denominator is $n(B)$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Out of 200 people, 80 swim and 30 swim \& run: } \Pr(\text{Run} \mid \text{Swim}) = \frac{30}{200} \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\Pr(\text{Run} \mid \text{Swim}) = \frac{n(\text{Run} \cap \text{Swim})}{n(\text{Swim})} = \frac{30}{80} = \frac{3}{8}$$
- **Counter-Example**: $\frac{30}{200}$ is the probability that a random person from the whole survey both swims and runs, not the proportion among swimmers.

#### Question `9_9_2_Q1` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
A biased coin is tossed $250$ times and lands on Heads $145$ times. (a) Find the relative frequency of Heads as a simplified fraction and a decimal. (b) How many Heads would you expect in $1000$ tosses of this coin?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Divide frequency by total trials: $\hat{p} = \frac{145}{250} = \frac{29}{50} = 0.58$.
2. Multiply by $1000$ future trials: $1000 \times 0.58 = 580$ Heads.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{29}{50} = 0.58$, (b) $580$ Heads

#### Question `9_9_2_Q2` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
Two events $A$ and $B$ have $\Pr(A) = 0.45$, $\Pr(B) = 0.35$, and $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0.15$. Find: (a) $\Pr(A \cup B)$ (inclusive 'or'), and (b) $\Pr(A \text{ xor } B)$ (exclusive 'or').

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By the Addition Rule, $\Pr(A \cup B) = 0.45 + 0.35 - 0.15 = 0.65$.
2. For exclusive 'or', subtract $\Pr(A \cap B)$ from $\Pr(A \cup B)$: $0.65 - 0.15 = 0.50$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $0.65$, (b) $0.50$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q3` [Medium] *(Fluency)*
A card is drawn at random from a standard deck of $52$ playing cards. Find the probability that the card is: (a) a Queen OR a Spade (inclusive 'or'), and (b) a Queen OR a Spade, but not the Queen of Spades (exclusive 'or').

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. There are $4$ Queens, $13$ Spades, and $1$ Queen of Spades: $\Pr(Q \cup S) = \frac{4 + 13 - 1}{52} = \frac{16}{52} = \frac{4}{13}$.
2. Removing the $1$ overlapping Queen of Spades leaves $\frac{16 - 1}{52} = \frac{15}{52}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{4}{13}$, (b) $\frac{15}{52}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q4` [Medium] *(Understanding)*
Given $\Pr(A) = 0.6$, $\Pr(B) = 0.5$, and $\Pr(A \cup B) = 0.85$, find $\Pr(A \cap B)$ and the conditional probability $\Pr(A \mid B)$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Rearrange the Addition Rule: $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cup B) = 0.6 + 0.5 - 0.85 = 0.25$.
2. Divide by $\Pr(B)$: $\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{0.25}{0.5} = 0.5$.

**Final Answer**: $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0.25,\; \Pr(A \mid B) = 0.5$
</details>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `9_9_2_Q5` [Medium] *(School Language Enrolment)*
At a Geelong secondary college, $120$ Year 9 students were asked whether they study French ($F$) or Japanese ($J$). Exactly $54$ study French, $42$ study Japanese, and $36$ study neither language. (a) How many students study both languages? (b) What is the probability that a randomly selected Japanese student also studies French?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $36$ study neither, $n(F \cup J) = 120 - 36 = 84$. By the Addition Rule, $n(F \cap J) = 54 + 42 - 84 = 12$ students.
2. Among the $42$ Japanese students, $12$ also study French: $\Pr(F \mid J) = \frac{12}{42} = \frac{2}{7}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $12$ students, (b) $\frac{2}{7}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q6` [Easy] *(Pooling Experimental Data)*
Three students test the same spinner to estimate the probability of landing on Red. Aria spins $20$ times ($7$ Red), Ben spins $80$ times ($26$ Red), and Chloe spins $100$ times ($31$ Red). (a) Which individual student has the most reliable estimate? (b) Pool all three datasets together to calculate the best overall relative frequency estimate of landing on Red.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Chloe's individual estimate is most reliable because she conducted the largest number of trials ($n = 100$).
2. Pooling all $20 + 80 + 100 = 200$ spins gives $\frac{7 + 26 + 31}{200} = \frac{64}{200} = \frac{8}{25} = 0.32$.

**Final Answer**: (a) Chloe ($n = 100$), (b) $\frac{64}{200} = \frac{8}{25} = 0.32$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q7` [Challenging] *(Simulation Design (VC2M9P03))*
A multiple-choice quiz has $4$ questions, each with $5$ options ($A, B, C, D, E$) of which only $1$ is correct ($\Pr(\text{correct}) = 0.2$). A student designs a simulation using random single digits $0\text{--}9$, where digits $0$ and $1$ represent a correct guess and digits $2\text{--}9$ represent an incorrect guess. Explain why this digit assignment is valid, and find the theoretical probability that a student who guesses all $4$ questions gets at least $1$ question correct.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. There are $10$ equally likely digits ($0\text{--}9$), and assigning $2$ digits ($0, 1$) to 'correct' gives $\Pr(\text{correct}) = \frac{2}{10} = 0.2 = \frac{1}{5}$, matching the $5$-option quiz.
2. Using the complement rule across $4$ independent questions: $\Pr(\text{at least } 1\text{ correct}) = 1 - \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^4 = 1 - \frac{256}{625} = \frac{369}{625} = 0.5904$.

**Final Answer**: $1 - \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^4 = 1 - \frac{256}{625} = \frac{369}{625} = 0.5904$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q8` [Challenging] *(Medical Diagnostics & False Positives)*
A medical rapid test is trialled on $500$ patients, of whom $100$ actually have a virus ($V$) and $400$ do not ($V'$). Among the $100$ infected patients, $92$ test positive ($+$). Among the $400$ uninfected patients, $28$ falsely test positive ($+$). If a patient tests positive, what is the probability (relative frequency) that they actually have the virus?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The total number of patients who test positive is $92 + 28 = 120$.
2. Of those $120$ positive results, $92$ actually have the virus: $\Pr(V \mid +) = \frac{92}{120} = \frac{23}{30} \approx 0.767$.

**Final Answer**: $\frac{92}{120} = \frac{23}{30} \approx 0.767$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `9_9_2_Q9` [Medium] *(Inclusive vs Exclusive Logic)*
Using a Venn diagram or algebra, explain why $\Pr(A \text{ xor } B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - 2\Pr(A \cap B)$ for exclusive 'or', whereas $\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$ for inclusive 'or'. Under what condition are inclusive 'or' and exclusive 'or' equal?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Only $A$ is $\Pr(A) - \Pr(A \cap B)$ and only $B$ is $\Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$; summing these two disjoint regions gives $\Pr(A \text{ xor } B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - 2\Pr(A \cap B)$.
2. Comparing with $\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B)$, the two expressions are equal if and only if $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0$ (mutually exclusive events).

**Final Answer**: They are equal if and only if $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0$ (when $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive)
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q10` [Challenging] *(Critiquing Probability Fallacies)*
A game-show host claims that if you roll a fair $6$-sided die $6$ times, you are guaranteed ($100\%$) to roll at least one $6$ because $\frac{1}{6} \times 6 = 1$. Critique this reasoning and calculate the true probability of rolling at least one $6$ in $6$ rolls (to $3$ decimal places).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Separate rolls are independent, not mutually exclusive, so you cannot add $\frac{1}{6}$ six times (otherwise $7$ rolls would give probability $\frac{7}{6} > 1$).
2. The true probability of at least one $6$ is $1 - \Pr(\text{no } 6\text{s}) = 1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^6 = 1 - \frac{15\,625}{46\,656} = \frac{31\,031}{46\,656} \approx 0.665$.

**Final Answer**: False: $\frac{1}{6} \times 6 = 1$ is the expected average number of $6$s, not the probability; true probability is $1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^6 = \frac{31\,031}{46\,656} \approx 0.665$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q11` [Extension] *(Fréchet Probability Bounds)*
In a school of $240$ students, $\Pr(A) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\Pr(B) = \frac{2}{3}$. Since $\Pr(A \cup B) \le 1$ and $\Pr(A \cap B) \le \min(\Pr(A), \Pr(B))$, find the minimum and maximum possible values of $\Pr(A \cap B)$ and the corresponding minimum and maximum number of students in $A \cap B$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $\Pr(A \cup B) = \Pr(A) + \Pr(B) - \Pr(A \cap B) \le 1$, we have $\Pr(A \cap B) \ge \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{3} - 1 = \frac{1}{6}$ ($40$ students).
2. Since $A \cap B \subseteq A$, $\Pr(A \cap B) \le \Pr(A) = \frac{1}{2}$ ($120$ students). Thus $40 \le n(A \cap B) \le 120$.

**Final Answer**: $\frac{1}{6} \le \Pr(A \cap B) \le \frac{1}{2}$ (between $40$ and $120$ students)
</details>

#### Question `9_9_2_Q12` [Extension] *(Monte Carlo Estimation of Pi)*
To estimate $\pi$ using a Monte Carlo simulation, a computer generates random points $(x, y)$ uniformly inside a $2 \times 2$ square (area $4$) containing an inscribed circle of radius $r = 1$ (area $\pi r^2 = \pi$). Out of $2000$ random points, $1571$ fall inside the circle ($x^2 + y^2 \le 1$). Use the relative frequency to estimate $\pi$ to $3$ decimal places.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The theoretical probability that a random point lands inside the circle is $\frac{\text{Area of circle}}{\text{Area of square}} = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
2. Equating this to the relative frequency gives $\frac{\hat{\pi}}{4} = \frac{1571}{2000} \implies \hat{\pi} = 4 \times 0.7855 = 3.142$.

**Final Answer**: $\hat{\pi} = 4 \times \frac{1571}{2000} = 3.142$
</details>
