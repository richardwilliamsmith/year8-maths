### Key Vocabulary
- **Multi-stage (compound) experiment**: A chance experiment consisting of two or more sequential or simultaneous steps, such as rolling two dice, tossing three coins, or drawing two marbles from a bag.
- **With replacement vs without replacement**: Sampling 'with replacement' returns the first item to the pool before the second draw (leaving totals and probabilities unchanged); sampling 'without replacement' keeps the first item out (reducing both the total number of items and the count of that item by $1$).
- **Sample space array (lattice diagram)**: A two-dimensional grid displaying all ordered pairs $(a, b)$ of outcomes for a two-step experiment; when the single-step outcomes are equally likely, the main diagonal of $(a, a)$ pairs is deleted if selection is without replacement.
- **Probability tree diagram**: A branching diagram showing sequential stages of an experiment where branch probabilities are multiplied along each path ('AND') and mutually exclusive path probabilities are added ('OR').

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Sample Space Arrays (Tables) With and Without Replacement

For a two-step experiment where step 1 has $m$ equally likely outcomes and step 2 has $n$ equally likely outcomes, a 2D array (grid) organises every ordered pair. If an item is selected twice WITH replacement from a set of $n$ items, there are $n \times n = n^2$ equally likely outcomes. If the two selections are made WITHOUT replacement, an item cannot be chosen twice in a row, so the $n$ diagonal entries $(A, A), (B, B), \dots$ are crossed out, leaving $n(n - 1)$ equally likely outcomes.

$$N_{\text{with replacement}} = n^2, \qquad N_{\text{without replacement}} = n(n - 1)$$

> **💡 Cross Out the Diagonal When Sampling Without Replacement**
> In a 2D grid of individual items, 'without replacement' means the exact same physical item cannot be picked on both draws — always cross out the main diagonal of identical pairs $(X_1, X_1)$ before counting.

### Tree Diagrams and Multiplying Along Branches

When outcomes are not equally likely (for example, a bag with $5$ red and $3$ blue marbles), instead of drawing a huge grid of $8 \times 8 = 64$ individual marbles, we draw a grouped probability tree diagram with just $2$ branches ('Red' and 'Blue') at each stage and write the probability fraction on every branch. To find the probability of a specific sequence of outcomes $(A \text{ then } B)$, multiply the probabilities along that path; to combine several favourable paths, add their endpoint probabilities.

$$\Pr(A \text{ then } B) = \Pr(A) \times \Pr(B \mid A), \qquad \Pr(\text{Event}) = \sum \Pr(\text{favourable paths})$$

> **💡 Multiply Along Branches (AND); Add Down the End Column (OR)**
> Moving left-to-right ALONG a branch sequence means 'Step 1 AND Step 2' ($\times$). Combining separate branch endings at the right-hand side means 'Path 1 OR Path 2' ($+$).

### Adjusting Denominators and Numerators Without Replacement

In a tree diagram WITHOUT replacement from a pool of $N$ items containing $a$ items of type $A$ and $b$ items of type $B$: on Stage 1, $\Pr(A_1) = \frac{a}{N}$. On Stage 2, only $N - 1$ items remain in total. Along the top branch (where $A$ was already removed), only $a - 1$ items of type $A$ remain, so $\Pr(A_2 \mid A_1) = \frac{a - 1}{N - 1}$ while $\Pr(B_2 \mid A_1) = \frac{b}{N - 1}$. At every single branching node, the branch probabilities leaving that node must sum to $1$.

$$\Pr(A_1 \cap A_2) = \frac{a}{N} \times \frac{a - 1}{N - 1}, \qquad \Pr(A_1 \cap B_2) = \frac{a}{N} \times \frac{b}{N - 1}$$

> **💡 Reduce the Denominator by 1 on Every Second-Stage Branch**
> When drawing without replacement, every Stage 2 fraction has denominator $N - 1$. Only the colour that was actually drawn in Stage 1 has its numerator reduced by $1$!

## Worked Examples

### WE9_9_1_1: Using a Sample Space Array for a Two-Step Experiment

**Problem**: Two letters are chosen at random from the word $\text{MATH}$ ($4$ distinct letters). Using a sample space array, find the probability that at least one vowel ($\text{A}$) is chosen if the selection is made: (a) WITH replacement, and (b) WITHOUT replacement.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | With replacement, there are $4 \times 4 = 16$ equally likely ordered pairs. Count the pairs containing $\text{A}$: row $\text{A}$ has $4$ pairs $(\text{AA}, \text{AM}, \text{AT}, \text{AH})$ and column $\text{A}$ has $3$ additional pairs $(\text{MA}, \text{TA}, \text{HA})$, giving $7$ outcomes. | $$\Pr(\text{at least one A, with replacement}) = \frac{7}{16}$$ |
| 2 | Without replacement, cross out the $4$ diagonal pairs $(\text{MM}, \text{AA}, \text{TT}, \text{HH})$, leaving $4 \times 3 = 12$ outcomes. Removing $(\text{AA})$ from the $7$ favourable pairs leaves $6$ outcomes $(\text{AM}, \text{AT}, \text{AH}, \text{MA}, \text{TA}, \text{HA})$. | $$\Pr(\text{at least one A, without replacement}) = \frac{6}{12} = \frac{1}{2}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{7}{16}$, (b) $\frac{1}{2}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: A fair $4$-sided die (numbered $1, 2, 3, 4$) is rolled twice. Find the probability that the sum of the two rolls is at least $6$.
> *Hint*: Out of $4 \times 4 = 16$ pairs, list those with sum $6, 7$, or $8$: $(2,4), (3,3), (4,2), (3,4), (4,3), (4,4)$.
> **Working**: There are $6$ favourable pairs out of $16$ total outcomes: $\Pr(\text{sum} \ge 6) = \frac{6}{16} = \frac{3}{8}$.
> **Answer**: $\frac{3}{8}$

### WE9_9_1_2: Tree Diagram Without Replacement (Box of Chocolates)

**Problem**: A box contains $6$ dark chocolates ($D$) and $4$ milk chocolates ($M$). Two chocolates are selected at random and eaten (without replacement). Find the probability of selecting: (a) two dark chocolates, (b) one chocolate of each type (in either order), and (c) at least one milk chocolate.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | There are $10$ chocolates initially ($\Pr(D_1) = \frac{6}{10},\; \Pr(M_1) = \frac{4}{10}$). For the second draw, $9$ chocolates remain. Multiply along the $DD$ branch for part (a). | $$\Pr(DD) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac{1}{3}$$ |
| 2 | For part (b), add the two mutually exclusive paths $DM$ and $MD$. | $$\Pr(\text{one of each}) = \Pr(DM) + \Pr(MD) = \left(\frac{6}{10} \times \frac{4}{9}\right) + \left(\frac{4}{10} \times \frac{6}{9}\right) = \frac{24}{90} + \frac{24}{90} = \frac{48}{90} = \frac{8}{15}$$ |
| 3 | For part (c), use the complement rule: $\Pr(\text{at least one } M) = 1 - \Pr(\text{no } M) = 1 - \Pr(DD)$. | $$\Pr(\text{at least one } M) = 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{1}{3}$, (b) $\frac{8}{15}$, (c) $\frac{2}{3}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: A bag holds $5$ red marbles and $3$ green marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement. Find the probability that both marbles are the same colour.
> *Hint*: Calculate $\Pr(RR) + \Pr(GG) = \left(\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{4}{7}\right) + \left(\frac{3}{8} \times \frac{2}{7}\right)$.
> **Working**: $\Pr(RR) + \Pr(GG) = \frac{20}{56} + \frac{6}{56} = \frac{26}{56} = \frac{13}{28}$.
> **Answer**: $\frac{13}{28}$

### WE9_9_1_3: Comparing Three-Stage Independent Events vs Conditional Probability

**Problem**: A Melbourne basketball player has a constant probability of $p = \frac{3}{4}$ of making any free throw (independent trials). If she takes $3$ free throws, find the probability that she makes: (a) all $3$ shots, and (b) exactly $2$ out of $3$ shots.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Let $S$ be a successful shot ($\Pr(S) = \frac{3}{4}$) and $F$ be a miss ($\Pr(F) = \frac{1}{4}$). Multiply $\frac{3}{4}$ three times for $(SSS)$. | $$\Pr(SSS) = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{27}{64}$$ |
| 2 | There are $3$ mutually exclusive branch sequences with exactly $2$ successes: $SSF$, $SFS$, and $FSS$. Each has probability $\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^2 \left(\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{9}{64}$. | $$\Pr(\text{exactly } 2) = 3 \times \left(\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{1}{4}\right) = 3 \times \frac{9}{64} = \frac{27}{64}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{27}{64}$, (b) $\frac{27}{64}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Using the same basketball player ($\Pr(S) = \frac{3}{4}$), find the probability that she makes at least $1$ of her $3$ free throws.
> *Hint*: Use the complement $1 - \Pr(FFF)$ where $\Pr(F) = \frac{1}{4}$.
> **Working**: $\Pr(FFF) = \left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^3 = \frac{1}{64}$, so $\Pr(\text{at least } 1) = 1 - \frac{1}{64} = \frac{63}{64}$.
> **Answer**: $\frac{63}{64}$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Forgetting to Reduce the Denominator on the Second Draw Without Replacement
- **The Misconception**: Keeping the denominator unchanged on the second stage of a tree diagram when sampling without replacement (e.g., writing $\frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{10}$ instead of $\frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9}$).
- **The Correct Concept**: When the first item is not replaced, both the number of remaining items of that colour AND the total number of items in the bag decrease by $1$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{6 Red, 4 Blue (without replacement): } \Pr(RR) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{10} = \frac{30}{100} \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\Pr(RR) = \frac{6}{10} \times \frac{5}{9} = \frac{30}{90} = \frac{1}{3}$$
- **Counter-Example**: With $\frac{5}{10}$ and $\frac{4}{10}$ on the second stage, the branch probabilities $\frac{5}{10} + \frac{4}{10} = \frac{9}{10} \neq 1$ fail to sum to $1$.

### Adding Instead of Multiplying Probabilities Along a Single Branch
- **The Misconception**: Adding the Stage 1 and Stage 2 probabilities along a branch path (e.g., $\Pr(HH) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$).
- **The Correct Concept**: Probabilities along a sequence of stages represent 'Stage 1 AND Stage 2', which requires multiplication ($\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$).
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\Pr(\text{Head then Head}) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1 \quad \text{(Incorrect — implies } HH \text{ is certain!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\Pr(\text{Head then Head}) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$$
- **Counter-Example**: Tossing two Heads in a row is obviously not a $100\%$ certainty; it happens in $1$ out of $4$ equally likely outcomes ($HH, HT, TH, TT$).

### Fluency & Understanding

#### Question `9_9_1_Q1` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
Two fair $6$-sided dice are rolled simultaneously. Using a $6 \times 6$ lattice array of $36$ outcomes, find the probability of rolling: (a) a double (both dice show the same number), and (b) a sum of $9$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. There are $6$ doubles $(1,1), (2,2), \dots, (6,6)$ out of $36$ outcomes, so $\Pr(\text{double}) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.
2. There are $4$ outcomes that sum to $9$: $(3,6), (4,5), (5,4), (6,3)$, so $\Pr(\text{sum } 9) = \frac{4}{36} = \frac{1}{9}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{1}{6}$, (b) $\frac{1}{9}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q2` [Easy] *(Fluency)*
A bag contains $7$ blue counters and $5$ yellow counters. Two counters are drawn at random WITH replacement. Find the probability that: (a) both are blue, and (b) the first is blue and the second is yellow.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. With replacement, $\Pr(B) = \frac{7}{12}$ and $\Pr(Y) = \frac{5}{12}$ on every draw, so $\Pr(BB) = \frac{7}{12} \times \frac{7}{12} = \frac{49}{144}$.
2. $\Pr(BY) = \frac{7}{12} \times \frac{5}{12} = \frac{35}{144}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{49}{144}$, (b) $\frac{35}{144}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q3` [Medium] *(Fluency)*
The same bag of $7$ blue and $5$ yellow counters now has two counters drawn WITHOUT replacement. Find the probability that: (a) both are blue, and (b) one is blue and one is yellow (in either order).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Without replacement, $\Pr(BB) = \frac{7}{12} \times \frac{6}{11} = \frac{42}{132} = \frac{7}{22}$.
2. $\Pr(BY \text{ or } YB) = \left(\frac{7}{12} \times \frac{5}{11}\right) + \left(\frac{5}{12} \times \frac{7}{11}\right) = \frac{70}{132} = \frac{35}{66}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{7}{22}$, (b) $\frac{35}{66}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q4` [Easy] *(Understanding)*
Three fair coins are tossed. List all $8$ outcomes in the sample space and find the probability of obtaining: (a) exactly $2$ Heads, and (b) at least $1$ Tail.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The $8$ equally likely outcomes are $\{HHH, HHT, HTH, THH, HTT, THT, TTH, TTT\}$. Exactly $3$ outcomes ($HHT, HTH, THH$) have $2$ Heads, giving $\frac{3}{8}$.
2. Every outcome except $HHH$ has at least $1$ Tail, giving $1 - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{7}{8}$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{3}{8}$, (b) $\frac{7}{8}$
</details>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `9_9_1_Q5` [Medium] *(Everyday Probability)*
A sock drawer contains $8$ black school socks and $4$ white sports socks loose in the dark. Liam grabs $2$ socks at random without replacement. What is the probability that he gets a matching pair (either both black or both white)?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Calculate $\Pr(BB) = \frac{8}{12} \times \frac{7}{11} = \frac{56}{132}$ and $\Pr(WW) = \frac{4}{12} \times \frac{3}{11} = \frac{12}{132}$.
2. Add the two mutually exclusive matching outcomes: $\Pr(\text{matching pair}) = \frac{56 + 12}{132} = \frac{68}{132} = \frac{17}{33}$.

**Final Answer**: $\frac{17}{33}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q6` [Medium] *(Public Transport Reliability)*
On any weekday morning in Melbourne, the probability that Tram $A$ arrives on time is $0.8$, and independently the probability that connecting Bus $B$ arrives on time is $0.9$. Find the probability that on a given morning: (a) both services arrive on time, and (b) exactly one of the two services is delayed.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Multiply the independent probabilities for part (a): $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0.8 \times 0.9 = 0.72$.
2. For exactly one delayed: $\Pr(A \cap B') + \Pr(A' \cap B) = (0.8 \times 0.1) + (0.2 \times 0.9) = 0.08 + 0.18 = 0.26$.

**Final Answer**: (a) $0.72$, (b) $0.26$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q7` [Challenging] *(Quality Control Sampling)*
A quality-control inspector at an electronics factory tests a batch of $15$ circuit boards, of which $3$ are defective ($D$) and $12$ are good ($G$). Three boards are selected at random WITHOUT replacement. Find the probability that none of the $3$ selected boards are defective.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. For all $3$ boards to be good ($GGG$), multiply the sequential probabilities without replacement: $\Pr(GGG) = \frac{12}{15} \times \frac{11}{14} \times \frac{10}{13}$.
2. Simplify the product: $\frac{4}{5} \times \frac{11}{14} \times \frac{10}{13} = \frac{440}{910} = \frac{44}{91}$.

**Final Answer**: $\frac{44}{91}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q8` [Challenging] *(Sequential Transfer Probability)*
Two bags sit on a table. Bag $1$ contains $3$ red and $2$ blue balls; Bag $2$ contains $1$ red and $4$ blue balls. A ball is chosen at random from Bag $1$ and placed into Bag $2$, and then a ball is drawn at random from Bag $2$. Find the probability that the ball drawn from Bag $2$ is red.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Case 1 (Red transferred, $\Pr(R_1) = \frac{3}{5}$): Bag $2$ now has $2$ red and $4$ blue ($6$ balls), so $\Pr(R_2 \mid R_1) = \frac{2}{6}$. Path probability $= \frac{3}{5} \times \frac{2}{6} = \frac{6}{30}$.
2. Case 2 (Blue transferred, $\Pr(B_1) = \frac{2}{5}$): Bag $2$ now has $1$ red and $5$ blue ($6$ balls), so $\Pr(R_2 \mid B_1) = \frac{1}{6}$. Path probability $= \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{2}{30}$. Total $\Pr(R_2) = \frac{6 + 2}{30} = \frac{8}{30} = \frac{4}{15}$.

**Final Answer**: $\frac{4}{15}$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `9_9_1_Q9` [Challenging] *(Symmetry of Sampling Without Replacement)*
In a bag containing $R$ red marbles and $B$ blue marbles (total $N = R + B$), one marble is drawn and not replaced, and then a second marble is drawn. Prove algebraically that the unconditional probability that the SECOND marble is red ($\Pr(R_2) = \Pr(R_1 R_2) + \Pr(B_1 R_2)$) is still exactly $\frac{R}{N}$, the same as on the first draw!

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Sum the two paths ending in $R_2$: $\Pr(R_2) = \frac{R}{N}\frac{R - 1}{N - 1} + \frac{B}{N}\frac{R}{N - 1} = \frac{R(R - 1 + B)}{N(N - 1)}$.
2. Since $R + B = N$, the factor $(R - 1 + B) = N - 1$ cancels with $(N - 1)$ in the denominator, leaving $\Pr(R_2) = \frac{R}{N}$.

**Final Answer**: $\Pr(R_2) = \frac{R(R-1) + B R}{N(N-1)} = \frac{R(R - 1 + B)}{N(N-1)} = \frac{R(N-1)}{N(N-1)} = \frac{R}{N}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q10` [Medium] *(Complement Strategy in Multi-Stage Trees)*
Explain why $\Pr(\text{at least one } 6)$ when rolling a fair die $3$ times is quickest to calculate using the complement rule $1 - \Pr(\text{no } 6\text{s})$, and evaluate this exact probability.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. In a $3$-stage tree diagram with branches '$6$' and 'not $6$', $7$ of the $8$ paths contain at least one $6$, whereas only $1$ path has no $6$s at all.
2. Using the complement rule gives $1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^3 = 1 - \frac{125}{216} = \frac{91}{216}$ in a single step.

**Final Answer**: $1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^3 = 1 - \frac{125}{216} = \frac{91}{216}$
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q11` [Extension] *(Algebraic Probability Modelling)*
A bag contains $n$ red marbles and $2$ blue marbles. Two marbles are drawn without replacement, and the probability that both marbles are red is $\frac{2}{5}$. Form a quadratic equation in $n$ and solve it to find the number of red marbles $n$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Set up $\Pr(RR) = \frac{n}{n + 2} \times \frac{n - 1}{n + 1} = \frac{2}{5} \implies 5n(n - 1) = 2(n + 2)(n + 1)$.
2. Expand and factorise: $5n^2 - 5n = 2n^2 + 6n + 4 \implies 3n^2 - 11n - 4 = 0 \implies (3n + 1)(n - 4) = 0$. Since $n > 0$ is an integer, $n = 4$.

**Final Answer**: $n = 4$ red marbles
</details>

#### Question `9_9_1_Q12` [Extension] *(Historical Probability Paradox)*
Chevalier de Méré's problem: Which is more likely — rolling at least one $6$ in $4$ rolls of a single fair die, or rolling at least one double-$6$ in $24$ rolls of a pair of fair dice? Compare $1 - \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4$ with $\frac{1}{2}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. For $4$ rolls of a single die, $\Pr(\text{no } 6) = \left(\frac{5}{6}\right)^4 = \frac{625}{1296}$, so $\Pr(\text{at least one } 6) = 1 - \frac{625}{1296} = \frac{671}{1296} \approx 0.5177$.
2. Since $\frac{671}{1296} > \frac{648}{1296} = \frac{1}{2}$, rolling at least one $6$ in $4$ rolls is favourable ($> 50\%$), whereas $1 - \left(\frac{35}{36}\right)^{24} \approx 0.4914 < 50\%$.

**Final Answer**: Rolling at least one $6$ in $4$ rolls has probability $1 - \frac{625}{1296} = \frac{671}{1296} \approx 0.5177 > 0.5$ (more likely than $0.5$, whereas double-$6$ in $24$ rolls is $1 - (\frac{35}{36})^{24} \approx 0.4914 < 0.5$)
</details>
