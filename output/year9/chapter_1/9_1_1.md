### Key Vocabulary
- **Rational number**: Any number that can be expressed as a fraction $\frac{p}{q}$ where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$. Its decimal expansion either terminates or recurs.
- **Irrational number**: A real number that cannot be written as a ratio of two integers; its decimal expansion is infinite and non-recurring (for example, $\pi$ and $\sqrt{2}$).
- **Surd**: An irrational root of a rational number, such as $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{10}$, or $\sqrt[3]{5}$, whose exact value cannot be written as a fraction.
- **Simplest surd form**: A square-root surd $a\sqrt{b}$ where the integer radicand $b$ has no perfect square factors greater than $1$.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### The Real Number Hierarchy

The set of real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) represents every point on the continuous number line and splits into two mutually exclusive sets: the rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$) and the irrational numbers ($\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$). Inside the rationals sit the integers ($\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$) and the natural counting numbers ($\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$). Note that a square root of a perfect square, such as $\sqrt{49} = 7$, is rational, whereas the square root of a non-square integer, such as $\sqrt{7}$, is an irrational surd.

$$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}, \quad \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup (\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q})$$

> **💡 Not Every Root Is a Surd**
> Always evaluate roots of perfect squares first: $\sqrt{36} = 6$ and $\sqrt{\frac{9}{25}} = \frac{3}{5}$ are rational numbers, not surds.

### Locating Surds on the Real Number Line

Even though a surd has an infinite non-recurring decimal expansion, it occupies a single exact location on the number line. By constructing a right-angled triangle with integer legs $a$ and $b$ starting at the origin, Pythagoras' theorem guarantees a hypotenuse of exact length $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. Using a pair of compasses centred at $0$ to swing this hypotenuse down onto the positive axis plots $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ exactly.

$$c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \implies \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}, \quad \sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{5}$$

> **💡 Bounding Surds Between Consecutive Integers**
> To estimate $\sqrt{n}$ without a calculator, bracket $n$ between consecutive perfect squares: since $16 < 20 < 25$, taking square roots gives $4 < \sqrt{20} < 5$.

### Simplifying, Adding, and Multiplying Surds

For any positive real numbers $a$ and $b$, the square root of a product equals the product of the square roots. To write a surd $\sqrt{n}$ in simplest form, factor out the largest perfect square factor $k^2$ that divides $n$. Once surds are simplified, 'like surds' sharing the same radicand can be added or subtracted just like algebraic like terms.

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}, \quad \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}, \quad m\sqrt{c} \pm n\sqrt{c} = (m \pm n)\sqrt{c}$$

> **💡 Extract the Largest Perfect Square Factor**
> When simplifying $\sqrt{72}$, use the largest square factor $36$ ($\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$) rather than stopping at $\sqrt{4 \times 18} = 2\sqrt{18}$.

## Worked Examples

### WE9_1_1_1: Simplifying Square-Root Surds

**Problem**: Express each surd in simplest form: (a) $\sqrt{48}$ and (b) $3\sqrt{75}$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), identify the largest perfect square factor of $48$, which is $16$ since $48 = 16 \times 3$. | $$\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \times 3} = \sqrt{16} \times \sqrt{3} = 4\sqrt{3}$$ |
| 2 | In part (b), identify the largest perfect square factor of $75$, which is $25$ ($75 = 25 \times 3$), and multiply its square root by the outside coefficient $3$. | $$3\sqrt{75} = 3\sqrt{25 \times 3} = 3 \times 5\sqrt{3} = 15\sqrt{3}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $4\sqrt{3}$, (b) $15\sqrt{3}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Express in simplest surd form: (a) $\sqrt{50}$ and (b) $4\sqrt{27}$.
> *Hint*: Use the square factors $25$ for $50$ and $9$ for $27$.
> **Working**: (a) $\sqrt{25 \times 2} = 5\sqrt{2}$; (b) $4\sqrt{9 \times 3} = 4 \times 3\sqrt{3} = 12\sqrt{3}$.
> **Answer**: (a) $5\sqrt{2}$, (b) $12\sqrt{3}$

### WE9_1_1_2: Simplifying and Collecting Like Surds

**Problem**: Simplify $2\sqrt{18} + \sqrt{50} - \sqrt{8}$ in exact surd form.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Simplify each surd individually by extracting its largest perfect square factor: $18 = 9 \times 2$, $50 = 25 \times 2$, and $8 = 4 \times 2$. | $$2\sqrt{18} + \sqrt{50} - \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{9 \times 2} + \sqrt{25 \times 2} - \sqrt{4 \times 2}$$ |
| 2 | Evaluate the square roots of the perfect squares and combine the coefficients of the like surd $\sqrt{2}$. | $$= 6\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} - 2\sqrt{2} = (6 + 5 - 2)\sqrt{2} = 9\sqrt{2}$$ |

**Answer**: $9\sqrt{2}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Simplify $3\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27} - \sqrt{3}$.
> *Hint*: Write $\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$ and $\sqrt{27} = 3\sqrt{3}$.
> **Working**: $3(2\sqrt{3}) + 3\sqrt{3} - 1\sqrt{3} = 6\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{3} = 8\sqrt{3}$.
> **Answer**: $8\sqrt{3}$

### WE9_1_1_3: Exact Geometric Distances Using Surds

**Problem**: A square courtyard in Geelong has side length $6\text{ m}$. Find the exact length of its diagonal in simplest surd form, and state which two consecutive integers this length lies between.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Apply Pythagoras' theorem $d = \sqrt{6^2 + 6^2}$ to find the unsimplified surd length and compare $\sqrt{72}$ with nearby perfect squares $64$ and $81$. | $$d = \sqrt{6^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{72}\text{ m}, \quad \sqrt{64} < \sqrt{72} < \sqrt{81} \implies 8 < d < 9$$ |
| 2 | Factor out the largest perfect square $36$ from $72$ to write the exact length in simplest surd form. | $$d = \sqrt{36 \times 2} = 6\sqrt{2}\text{ m}$$ |

**Answer**: $6\sqrt{2}\text{ m}$, which lies between $8\text{ m}$ and $9\text{ m}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: A rectangle has length $8\text{ cm}$ and width $4\text{ cm}$. Find its exact diagonal length in simplest surd form and the two consecutive integers it lies between.
> *Hint*: Calculate $\sqrt{8^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{80}$ and factor out $16$.
> **Working**: $d = \sqrt{64 + 16} = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt{5}\text{ cm}$; since $64 < 80 < 81$, it lies between $8$ and $9$.
> **Answer**: $4\sqrt{5}\text{ cm}$, between $8\text{ cm}$ and $9\text{ cm}$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Adding Radicands Under Separate Square Roots
- **The Misconception**: Writing $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$, or claiming $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{5}$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Square roots do NOT distribute over addition or subtraction: $\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \neq \sqrt{a + b}$. Only like surds with the same radicand can be combined by adding their coefficients.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7 \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$, whereas $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \neq 7$.

### Confusing Any Square Root Symbol with an Irrational Surd
- **The Misconception**: Classifying $\sqrt{64}$ or $\sqrt{\frac{4}{9}}$ as irrational surds simply because they are written with a radical sign $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Whether a number is rational or irrational depends on its value, not how it is written. Because $\sqrt{64} = 8$ and $\sqrt{\frac{4}{9}} = \frac{2}{3}$, both are rational numbers.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\sqrt{64} \text{ has a root sign, so it is an irrational surd.} \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\sqrt{64} = 8 = \frac{8}{1} \in \mathbb{Q} \quad (\text{Rational, not a surd})$$
- **Counter-Example**: $\sqrt{49} = 7$ is a natural number, an integer, and a rational number.

#### Question `9_1_1_Q1` [Easy] *(Classifying Real Numbers)*
From the list $\left\{-\sqrt{36},\; \sqrt{15},\; 0.\overline{4},\; \pi,\; \sqrt{\frac{25}{49}}\right\}$, state which numbers are irrational and simplify the rational square roots.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Evaluate each square root where possible: $-\sqrt{36} = -6 \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\sqrt{\frac{25}{49}} = \frac{5}{7} \in \mathbb{Q}$.
2. Note that $0.\overline{4} = \frac{4}{9} \in \mathbb{Q}$. Since $15$ is not a perfect square, $\sqrt{15}$ and $\pi$ are irrational.

**Final Answer**: Irrational: $\sqrt{15}, \pi$; simplified rational roots: $-\sqrt{36} = -6$ and $\sqrt{\frac{25}{49}} = \frac{5}{7}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q2` [Easy] *(Simplifying Surds)*
Simplify the surd $\sqrt{108}$ into simplest surd form.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Find the largest perfect square factor of $108$: $108 = 36 \times 3$.
2. Write $\sqrt{108} = \sqrt{36} \times \sqrt{3} = 6\sqrt{3}$.

**Final Answer**: $6\sqrt{3}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q3` [Medium] *(Adding and Subtracting Like Surds)*
Simplify $5\sqrt{20} - 2\sqrt{45} + \sqrt{80}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Simplify each surd: $5\sqrt{4 \times 5} = 10\sqrt{5}$, $2\sqrt{9 \times 5} = 6\sqrt{5}$, and $\sqrt{16 \times 5} = 4\sqrt{5}$.
2. Combine the like surds: $10\sqrt{5} - 6\sqrt{5} + 4\sqrt{5} = 8\sqrt{5}$.

**Final Answer**: $8\sqrt{5}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q4` [Medium] *(Multiplying Surds)*
Multiply and simplify $3\sqrt{6} \times 2\sqrt{15}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Multiply the outside coefficients and the radicands: $(3 \times 2)\sqrt{6 \times 15} = 6\sqrt{90}$.
2. Extract the largest square factor $9$ from $90$: $6\sqrt{9 \times 10} = 6 \times 3\sqrt{10} = 18\sqrt{10}$.

**Final Answer**: $18\sqrt{10}$
</details>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `9_1_1_Q5` [Medium] *(Geometric Number Line Construction)*
To mark the exact point $\sqrt{13}$ on a number line starting at $0$, a student draws a horizontal segment of length $a$ units along the axis and a vertical segment of length $b$ units at right angles to it, where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers with $a > b$. Find $a$ and $b$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By Pythagoras' theorem, the hypotenuse has length $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{13}$, so $a^2 + b^2 = 13$.
2. Testing positive integer squares less than $13$ ($1, 4, 9$) gives $9 + 4 = 13$, so $a = 3$ and $b = 2$.

**Final Answer**: $a = 3, b = 2$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q6` [Medium] *(Perimeter and Area with Surds)*
A rectangular vegetable garden in Bendigo has length $5\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$ and width $2\sqrt{12}\text{ m}$. Find both the exact perimeter (in simplest surd form) and the exact area of the garden.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. First simplify the width: $2\sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{4 \times 3} = 4\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$.
2. Perimeter $P = 2(5\sqrt{3} + 4\sqrt{3}) = 2(9\sqrt{3}) = 18\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$.
3. Area $A = 5\sqrt{3} \times 4\sqrt{3} = 20 \times 3 = 60\text{ m}^2$.

**Final Answer**: Perimeter $= 18\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$; Area $= 60\text{ m}^2$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q7` [Medium] *(Engineering Exact Lengths)*
A guy wire is attached to the top of a vertical $12\text{ m}$ mast and anchored to level ground $6\text{ m}$ from the base of the mast. Find the exact length of the wire in simplest surd form.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By Pythagoras' theorem, $L = \sqrt{12^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{144 + 36} = \sqrt{180}\text{ m}$.
2. Extract the largest square factor $36$: $\sqrt{180} = \sqrt{36 \times 5} = 6\sqrt{5}\text{ m}$.

**Final Answer**: $6\sqrt{5}\text{ m}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q8` [Challenging] *(Exact Altitude and Area)*
An equilateral triangle has side length $8\text{ cm}$. An altitude drawn from the top vertex bisects the base into two $4\text{ cm}$ segments. Find the exact perpendicular height $h$ in simplest surd form, and hence find the exact area of the equilateral triangle.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Apply Pythagoras' theorem to half of the equilateral triangle: $h = \sqrt{8^2 - 4^2} = \sqrt{64 - 16} = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3}\text{ cm}$.
2. Calculate the area: $A = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times 8 \times 4\sqrt{3} = 16\sqrt{3}\text{ cm}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $h = 4\sqrt{3}\text{ cm}$; Area $= 16\sqrt{3}\text{ cm}^2$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `9_1_1_Q9` [Medium] *(Ordering Surds Exactly)*
Without using a calculator, determine which is larger: $4\sqrt{3}$ or $3\sqrt{5}$. Justify your answer by rewriting each number as the square root of a single integer.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Write each outside factor inside the square root: $4\sqrt{3} = \sqrt{16 \times 3} = \sqrt{48}$ and $3\sqrt{5} = \sqrt{9 \times 5} = \sqrt{45}$.
2. Since $48 > 45$ and the square root function is strictly increasing, $\sqrt{48} > \sqrt{45}$, so $4\sqrt{3} > 3\sqrt{5}$.

**Final Answer**: $4\sqrt{3}$ is larger because $\sqrt{48} > \sqrt{45}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q10` [Medium] *(Closure of Irrational Numbers)*
A student claims that the product of two irrational surds is always irrational. Disprove this claim with a counter-example and state a condition under which $\sqrt{a} \times \sqrt{b}$ is rational.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Choose two surds whose radicands multiply to a perfect square, such as $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8}$.
2. Then $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4$, which is rational. In general, $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ is rational whenever $ab$ is a perfect square.

**Final Answer**: Counter-example: $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4 \in \mathbb{Q}$; rational whenever $ab$ is a perfect square
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q11` [Challenging] *(Rationalising the Denominator)*
To eliminate the surd from the denominator of $\frac{12}{\sqrt{3}}$ (called rationalising the denominator), multiply both the numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{3}$ and simplify.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{3}$: $\frac{12}{\sqrt{3}} \times \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{12\sqrt{3}}{3}$.
2. Divide $12$ by $3$ to obtain $4\sqrt{3}$.

**Final Answer**: $4\sqrt{3}$
</details>

#### Question `9_1_1_Q12` [Extension] *(Spiral of Theodorus)*
In the Spiral of Theodorus, the first right-angled triangle has legs of $1\text{ cm}$ and $1\text{ cm}$ (hypotenuse $\sqrt{2}\text{ cm}$), and each subsequent triangle uses the previous hypotenuse as one leg and a perpendicular segment of $1\text{ cm}$ as the other leg. Find the hypotenuse of the $17\text{th}$ triangle in simplest surd form.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The $1\text{st}$ triangle has hypotenuse $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2}$, the $2\text{nd}$ has $\sqrt{3}$, and by induction the $n\text{th}$ triangle has hypotenuse $\sqrt{n + 1}\text{ cm}$.
2. For $n = 17$, the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{17 + 1} = \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \times 2} = 3\sqrt{2}\text{ cm}$.

**Final Answer**: $3\sqrt{2}\text{ cm}$
</details>
