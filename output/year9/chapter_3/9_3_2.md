### Key Vocabulary
- **Highest Common Factor (HCF) extraction**: The mandatory first step in any factorisation problem: dividing out the largest numerical and algebraic factor shared by all terms.
- **Difference of two squares (DOTS) factorisation**: Rewriting a difference of two squared expressions $a^2 - b^2$ as the conjugate binomial product $(a - b)(a + b)$.
- **Complete factorisation**: Continuing to factorise each bracketed factor until no factor can be broken down any further.
- **Factorising by grouping in pairs**: Factorising a four-term expression $ac + ad + bc + bd$ by factoring the first pair as $a(c + d)$ and the second pair as $b(c + d)$, then extracting the common binomial factor $(c + d)$.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Factorising the Difference of Two Squares (DOTS)

Reversing the expansion $(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2$ gives the difference of two squares factorisation law. Whenever an expression consists of one squared term subtracted from another squared term, it factorises into the difference and sum of their square roots. This even applies over the real numbers when a constant is not a perfect square by using a surd: $x^2 - 7 = (x - \sqrt{7})(x + \sqrt{7})$.

$$a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b), \quad x^2 - k = (x - \sqrt{k})(x + \sqrt{k}) \; (k > 0)$$

> **💡 A Sum of Two Squares Does Not Factorise Over the Reals**
> Be careful: $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$, but $a^2 + b^2$ has a PLUS sign and cannot be factorised into real linear factors.

### Extracting a Common Factor Before Applying DOTS

Many expressions do not initially look like a difference of two squares until their Highest Common Factor (HCF) is taken out. For example, in $3x^2 - 27$, neither $3$ nor $27$ is a square number, but extracting the common factor $3$ gives $3(x^2 - 9) = 3(x - 3)(x + 3)$.

$$3x^2 - 27 = 3(x^2 - 9) = 3(x - 3)(x + 3)$$

> **💡 Golden Rule of Factorising: Always Check for an HCF First**
> Before trying any special identity, always inspect all terms and factor out the Highest Common Factor first.

### Factorising Four-Term Expressions by Grouping in Pairs

When an expression has four terms with no single factor common to all four, split the expression into two pairs of two terms. Factor out the HCF from the first pair and the HCF from the second pair so that both pairs leave the exact same bracketed binomial factor, then factor out that common binomial.

$$x^3 + 3x^2 + 4x + 12 = x^2(x + 3) + 4(x + 3) = (x + 3)(x^2 + 4)$$

> **💡 Watch the Sign When the Third Term Is Negative**
> In $x^2 + 5x - 2x - 10$, factor $-2$ out of the second pair so the sign inside flips to match: $x(x + 5) - 2(x + 5) = (x + 5)(x - 2)$.

## Worked Examples

### WE9_3_2_1: Factorising Differences of Two Squares (Including Surds)

**Problem**: Factorise completely: (a) $16x^2 - 81y^2$ and (b) $x^2 - 11$ over the real numbers.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), rewrite $16x^2 = (4x)^2$ and $81y^2 = (9y)^2$, then apply $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$. | $$16x^2 - 81y^2 = (4x)^2 - (9y)^2 = (4x - 9y)(4x + 9y)$$ |
| 2 | In part (b), write $11 = (\sqrt{11})^2$ and apply DOTS using surds. | $$x^2 - 11 = x^2 - (\sqrt{11})^2 = (x - \sqrt{11})(x + \sqrt{11})$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $(4x - 9y)(4x + 9y)$, (b) $(x - \sqrt{11})(x + \sqrt{11})$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Factorise completely: (a) $25a^2 - 49b^2$ and (b) $x^2 - 18$ (simplify any surd).
> *Hint*: In (b), $\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$.
> **Working**: (a) $(5a - 7b)(5a + 7b)$; (b) $(x - \sqrt{18})(x + \sqrt{18}) = (x - 3\sqrt{2})(x + 3\sqrt{2})$.
> **Answer**: (a) $(5a - 7b)(5a + 7b)$, (b) $(x - 3\sqrt{2})(x + 3\sqrt{2})$

### WE9_3_2_2: Multi-Step Factorisation: HCF Followed by DOTS

**Problem**: Factorise completely: (a) $5x^2 - 80$ and (b) $(x + 2)^2 - 25$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), extract the common factor $5$ first, then apply DOTS to $(x^2 - 16)$. | $$5x^2 - 80 = 5(x^2 - 16) = 5(x - 4)(x + 4)$$ |
| 2 | In part (b), treat $(x + 2)$ as the first squared term $a$ and $25 = 5^2$ as $b^2$, then simplify inside each bracket. | $$(x + 2)^2 - 5^2 = ((x + 2) - 5)((x + 2) + 5) = (x - 3)(x + 7)$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $5(x - 4)(x + 4)$, (b) $(x - 3)(x + 7)$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Factorise completely: (a) $2m^3 - 50m$ and (b) $(x - 4)^2 - 9$.
> *Hint*: In (a), extract $2m$ first.
> **Working**: (a) $2m(m^2 - 25) = 2m(m - 5)(m + 5)$; (b) $(x - 4 - 3)(x - 4 + 3) = (x - 7)(x - 1)$.
> **Answer**: (a) $2m(m - 5)(m + 5)$, (b) $(x - 7)(x - 1)$

### WE9_3_2_3: Factorising by Grouping in Pairs

**Problem**: Factorise completely: $2x^2 + 6x - 5x - 15$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Factor $2x$ out of the first two terms ($2x^2 + 6x$) and $-5$ out of the last two terms ($-5x - 15$) so both pairs reveal the common binomial $(x + 3)$. | $$2x^2 + 6x - 5x - 15 = 2x(x + 3) - 5(x + 3)$$ |
| 2 | Extract the common binomial factor $(x + 3)$. | $$= (x + 3)(2x - 5)$$ |

**Answer**: $(x + 3)(2x - 5)$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Factorise by grouping in pairs: $3x^2 - 12x - 4x + 16$.
> *Hint*: Factor $3x$ from the first pair and $-4$ from the second pair.
> **Working**: $3x(x - 4) - 4(x - 4) = (x - 4)(3x - 4)$.
> **Answer**: $(x - 4)(3x - 4)$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Attempting to Factorise a Sum of Two Squares as (a + b)(a + b)
- **The Misconception**: Factorising $x^2 + 9$ as $(x + 3)(x + 3)$ or $(x - 3)(x + 3)$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Expanding $(x + 3)^2$ gives $x^2 + 6x + 9 \neq x^2 + 9$, and $(x - 3)(x + 3) = x^2 - 9$. A sum of two squares $x^2 + a^2$ ($a \neq 0$) cannot be factorised over the real numbers.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$x^2 + 9 = (x + 3)(x + 3) \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$x^2 + 9 \text{ cannot be factorised over } \mathbb{R}. \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: For $x = 1$, $1^2 + 9 = 10$, whereas $(1 + 3)^2 = 16 \neq 10$.

### Forgetting to Take the Square Root of the Numerical Coefficient in DOTS
- **The Misconception**: Factorising $9x^2 - 16$ as $(9x - 4)(9x + 4)$ instead of $(3x - 4)(3x + 4)$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Since $9x^2 = (3x)^2$, the square root of the first term is $3x$, not $9x$: $9x^2 - 16 = (3x - 4)(3x + 4)$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$9x^2 - 16 = (9x - 4)(9x + 4) \quad (\text{Incorrect — expands to } 81x^2 - 16)$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$9x^2 - 16 = (3x)^2 - 4^2 = (3x - 4)(3x + 4) \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: If $x = 1$, $9(1)^2 - 16 = -7$ and $(3 - 4)(3 + 4) = -7$, whereas $(9 - 4)(9 + 4) = 65$.

1. Extract the common numerical factor $3$: $3(a^2 - 25b^2)$.
2. Apply DOTS to $a^2 - (5b)^2$: $3(a - 5b)(a + 5b)$.

**Final Answer**: $3(a - 5b)(a + 5b)$

Factorise by grouping in pairs: $ab + 4a - 3b - 12$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Factor the first pair by $a$ and the second pair by $-3$: $a(b + 4) - 3(b + 4)$.
2. Extract the common binomial factor $(b + 4)$: $(b + 4)(a - 3)$.

**Final Answer**: $(b + 4)(a - 3)$

#### Question `9_3_2_Q4` [Challenging] *(DOTS with Two Binomials)*
Factorise completely $(2x - 1)^2 - (x + 4)^2$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Apply $A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$ with $A = 2x - 1$ and $B = x + 4$: $((2x - 1) - (x + 4))((2x - 1) + (x + 4))$.
2. Simplify each bracket: $(x - 5)(3x + 3)$.
3. Extract the common factor $3$ from the second bracket: $3(x - 5)(x + 1)$.

**Final Answer**: $3(x - 5)(x + 1)$
</details>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `9_3_2_Q5` [Medium] *(Annulus Area via DOTS)*
A circular steel washer has outer radius $R = 6.8\text{ cm}$ and inner hole radius $r = 3.2\text{ cm}$. Factorise the area formula $A = \pi R^2 - \pi r^2$, and use the factorised form to calculate the exact area of the washer in terms of $\pi$ without squaring decimals.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Factor out $\pi$ and apply DOTS: $A = \pi(R^2 - r^2) = \pi(R - r)(R + r)$.
2. Substitute $R = 6.8$ and $r = 3.2$: $R - r = 3.6$ and $R + r = 10.0$, so $A = \pi(3.6)(10) = 36\pi\text{ cm}^2$.

**Final Answer**: $A = \pi(R - r)(R + r) = 36\pi\text{ cm}^2$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q6` [Medium] *(Solar Panel Dimensions)*
A rectangular solar panel in Mildura has an area of $(9x^2 - 16)\text{ m}^2$ and a length of $(3x + 4)\text{ m}$ (where $x > 2$). Find an algebraic expression for the width of the panel and hence find the perimeter of the panel in terms of $x$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Factorise the area: $9x^2 - 16 = (3x + 4)(3x - 4)$. Dividing by length $(3x + 4)$ gives width $= (3x - 4)\text{ m}$.
2. Calculate the perimeter: $P = 2((3x + 4) + (3x - 4)) = 2(6x) = 12x\text{ m}$.

**Final Answer**: Width $= (3x - 4)\text{ m}$; Perimeter $= 12x\text{ m}$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q7` [Medium] *(Pythagorean Triples via DOTS)*
A right-angled triangle has hypotenuse $c = 85\text{ cm}$ and one leg $a = 84\text{ cm}$. Use the factorised form of Pythagoras' theorem $b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = (c - a)(c + a)$ to find the length of the other leg $b$ mentally.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Substitute $c = 85$ and $a = 84$ into $b^2 = (c - a)(c + a) = (85 - 84)(85 + 84) = 1 \times 169 = 169$.
2. Take the positive square root: $b = \sqrt{169} = 13\text{ cm}$.

**Final Answer**: $b = 13\text{ cm}$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q8` [Challenging] *(3D Volume Factorisation by Grouping and DOTS)*
The volume of a rectangular shipping crate in Port Melbourne is given by the four-term polynomial $V = 2x^3 + 10x^2 - 18x - 90\text{ m}^3$ (for $x > 3$). Factorise $V$ completely into a constant and three linear factors representing the crate's dimensions.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Extract the common numerical factor $2$: $V = 2(x^3 + 5x^2 - 9x - 45)$.
2. Group in pairs inside the brackets: $2[x^2(x + 5) - 9(x + 5)] = 2(x + 5)(x^2 - 9)$.
3. Factorise $x^2 - 9$ by DOTS: $V = 2(x + 5)(x - 3)(x + 3)$.

**Final Answer**: $2(x + 5)(x - 3)(x + 3)$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q9` [Easy] *(Mental Evaluation of Large Squares)*
Use the difference of two squares identity to evaluate $2026^2 - 2024^2$ without calculating either square.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Apply $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$ with $a = 2026$ and $b = 2024$.

#### Question `9_3_2_Q10` [Medium] *(Repeated Difference of Two Squares)*
A student factorised $x^4 - 16$ as $(x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4)$ and stopped. Explain why this factorisation is incomplete and write the fully factorised form over the real numbers.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. While $x^2 + 4$ is a sum of two squares and cannot be factorised further over $\mathbb{R}$, the first factor $x^2 - 4 = x^2 - 2^2$ is another difference of two squares.
2. Factorising $x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$ gives the complete factorisation $(x - 2)(x + 2)(x^2 + 4)$.

**Final Answer**: $x^2 - 4$ is itself a difference of two squares; complete factorisation is $(x - 2)(x + 2)(x^2 + 4)$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q11` [Medium] *(DOTS with Simplified Surds)*
Factorise $x^2 - 20$ over the real numbers, writing any surds in simplest form.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Write $20 = (\sqrt{20})^2 = (2\sqrt{5})^2$.
2. Apply DOTS: $x^2 - 20 = (x - 2\sqrt{5})(x + 2\sqrt{5})$.

**Final Answer**: $(x - 2\sqrt{5})(x + 2\sqrt{5})$
</details>

#### Question `9_3_2_Q12` [Extension] *(Divisibility Proof Using DOTS)*
Prove that for any integer $n$, the difference between the squares of the next odd integer $(2n + 3)$ and the previous odd integer $(2n - 1)$ is always a multiple of $8$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Apply DOTS to $(2n + 3)^2 - (2n - 1)^2$: $((2n + 3) - (2n - 1))((2n + 3) + (2n - 1))$.
2. Simplify each bracket: $(4)(4n + 2) = 8(2n + 1)$. Since $2n + 1$ is an integer, $8(2n + 1)$ is a multiple of $8$.

**Final Answer**: $(2n + 3)^2 - (2n - 1)^2 = 4(4n + 2) = 8(2n + 1)$, which is always a multiple of $8$
</details>
