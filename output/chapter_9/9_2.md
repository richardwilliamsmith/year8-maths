### Key Vocabulary
- **Relative Frequency (Experimental Probability)**: The proportion of trials in which an event $E$ actually occurs during an experiment: $\text{RF}(E) = \frac{\text{frequency of } E}{\text{total number of trials}} = \frac{f}{n}$.
- **Expected Frequency**: The theoretical average number of times an event $E$ is predicted to occur in $n$ independent trials, calculated as $\text{Expected Frequency} = n \times P(E)$.
- **Law of Large Numbers**: The fundamental principle that as the number of independent trials $n$ increases, the experimental relative frequency $\frac{f}{n}$ tends to get closer and closer to the true theoretical probability $P(E)$.
- **Simulation**: A model of a real-world chance scenario using a physical or digital random generator (such as dice, spinners, or a spreadsheet `RANDBETWEEN` function) whose outcomes match the theoretical probabilities of the real event.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Relative Frequency and Experimental Probability

When a chance experiment is repeated $n$ times and an event $E$ occurs $f$ times, the **relative frequency** (or **experimental probability**) of $E$ is $\text{RF}(E) = \frac{f}{n}$. Across all mutually exclusive outcomes in a frequency table, the sum of all frequencies equals $n$, and the sum of all relative frequencies always equals $1$ (or $100\%$). When the true theoretical probability is unknown (such as the strike rate of a cricketer or the reliability of a manufactured component), relative frequency from a large sample provides our best estimate of $P(E)$.

$$\text{Relative Frequency } \hat{p} = \frac{f}{n}, \qquad \sum \text{RF} = 1$$

> **💡 Relative Frequency is a Fraction of the Total Trials**
> Always divide the event frequency $f$ by the **total number of trials** $n = \sum f$, never by the number of categories or rows in the table.

### Expected Frequency in Repeated Trials

If an event $E$ has probability $P(E)$ on a single trial and the experiment is repeated $n$ times independently, the **expected frequency** (expected number of occurrences) is $E(X) = n \times P(E)$. An expected frequency is a theoretical long-run average — it does not have to be a whole number, and in any single batch of $n$ trials the actual observed count will naturally fluctuate around $n \times P(E)$.

$$\text{Expected Frequency} = n \times P(E)$$

> **💡 Expected Does Not Mean Guaranteed!**
> If you toss a fair coin $20$ times, the expected number of Heads is $20 \times 0.5 = 10$, but observing $8, 9, 11$, or $12$ Heads is completely normal random variation.

### Sample Size and the Law of Large Numbers

In a small number of trials (such as $n = 10$ spins), sample-to-sample variation is large and the relative frequency $\frac{f}{n}$ can differ noticeably from the theoretical probability $P(E)$. As the number of trials increases to $n = 100$, $1000$, or $10\,000$, the **Law of Large Numbers** guarantees that random fluctuations average out and $\frac{f}{n}$ converges towards $P(E)$. If a large discrepancy persists even after thousands of trials, we have strong evidence that the device (e.g. a die or spinner) is biased.

$$\text{As } n \to \infty, \qquad \text{Relative Frequency } \frac{f}{n} \longrightarrow P(E)$$

> **💡 Trust the Largest Sample Size**
> When several students test the same mystery spinner with different numbers of spins, the student with the largest number of spins (or the combined total of all students' spins) gives the most reliable estimate of the true probability.

### Designing Digital and Physical Simulations

To simulate an event with probability $P(E) = \frac{a}{b}$ using a digital random integer generator `RANDBETWEEN(1, b)` (which chooses each integer from $1$ to $b$ with equal probability $\frac{1}{b}$), assign exactly $a$ integers to represent 'Success' ($E$) and the remaining $b - a$ integers to represent 'Failure' ($E'$). For two-step experiments, generate two random numbers per trial (one for each step) and count the proportion of simulated trials in which the compound event occurs.

$$\text{To simulate } P(E) = \frac{a}{b} \text{ using } \texttt{RANDBETWEEN(1, b)}: \quad \text{assign } 1, 2, \dots, a \text{ to } E$$

> **💡 Match the Ratio of Assigned Numbers to $P(E)$**
> To simulate a $70\%$ ($\frac{7}{10}$) free-throw shooter using `RANDBETWEEN(1, 10)`, assign integers $1$ to $7$ as 'Goal' and integers $8$ to $10$ as 'Miss'.

| Step | Thinking | Working |

### WE9_2_2: Pooling Trials to Estimate Unknown Probabilities in a Biased Spinner

**Problem**: Three students test a biased three-sector spinner (Red, White, Gold) to estimate the unknown proportion of the spinner's area that is Gold:
- Chloe spins $20$ times and gets $9$ Golds.
- Dev spins $80$ times and gets $26$ Golds.
- Ella spins $300$ times and gets $93$ Golds.
(a) Calculate each student's relative frequency of Gold as a decimal. (b) Combine all three students' results to find the pooled relative frequency of Gold across all $400$ spins. (c) If the spinner is divided into $25$ equal-sized sectors in total, estimate how many of those $25$ sectors are painted Gold.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Calculate $\frac{f}{n}$ for each student and then sum all Gold frequencies ($9 + 26 + 93$) divided by the total spins ($20 + 80 + 300 = 400$). | $$\text{(a) Chloe: } \frac{9}{20} = 0.45, \qquad \text{Dev: } \frac{26}{80} = 0.325, \qquad \text{Ella: } \frac{93}{300} = 0.31$$

**Answer**: (a) Chloe $0.45$, Dev $0.325$, Ella $0.31$; (b) Pooled relative frequency $= \frac{8}{25} = 0.32$; (c) $8$ Gold sectors out of $25$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### The Gambler's Fallacy (Believing Past Independent Trials Force an Immediate 'Correction')
- **The Misconception**: Thinking that if a fair coin has landed Tails $5$ times in a row, the $6$th toss is 'due' to be Heads and has a probability greater than $\frac{1}{2}$.
- **The Correct Concept**: A coin has no memory! Each toss is an independent event with $P(\text{Heads}) = \frac{1}{2}$. The Law of Large Numbers works by **diluting** early streaks over thousands of future trials, not by altering the probability of the next toss.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{After } T, T, T, T, T: \quad P(\text{6th toss is } H) > \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{(Incorrect — Gambler's Fallacy)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Each toss is independent: } P(\text{6th toss is } H) = \frac{1}{2}$$
- **Counter-Example**: Even if the first $5$ tosses are Tails ($0$ Heads in $5$), followed by $500$ Heads in the next $1000$ tosses, the overall relative frequency becomes $\frac{500}{1005} \approx 0.4975 \approx 0.5$ without any compensation occurring.

### Using an Unweighted Average of Relative Frequencies Across Unequal Sample Sizes
- **The Misconception**: Combining two experiments of different sizes (e.g. $n_1 = 10$ trials and $n_2 = 90$ trials) by averaging their two percentages $\frac{\text{RF}_1 + \text{RF}_2}{2}$ equally.
- **The Correct Concept**: A sample of $90$ trials carries $9$ times as much evidence as a sample of $10$ trials. To pool multiple experiments, always add the total successes and divide by the total number of trials: $\text{RF}_{\text{pooled}} = \frac{f_1 + f_2}{n_1 + n_2}$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Exp 1: } \frac{8}{10} = 0.80, \quad \text{Exp 2: } \frac{45}{90} = 0.50 \implies \frac{0.80 + 0.50}{2} = 0.65 \quad \text{(Incorrect)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Pooled RF} = \frac{8 + 45}{10 + 90} = \frac{53}{100} = 0.53$$
- **Counter-Example**: Out of $100$ total trials, the event occurred $8 + 45 = 53$ times ($53\%$), not $65$ times!

#### Question `9_2_Q6` [Challenging] *(Coupon Collector Simulation)*
A cereal company places one of $4$ collectible Matildas player cards at random into each box (each player equally likely, $P = \frac{1}{4}$). A student simulates buying $3$ boxes using `RANDBETWEEN(1, 4)` three times per trial to estimate the probability of getting **all 3 cards different**. (a) Calculate the exact theoretical probability that $3$ boxes contain $3$ different player cards. (b) In $160$ simulated trials, $3$ different cards occurred $64$ times. Compare the simulated relative frequency with the theoretical probability.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The first box gives any card ($\frac{4}{4} = 1$). The second box must be one of the remaining $3$ cards ($\frac{3}{4}$), and the third box must be one of the remaining $2$ cards ($\frac{2}{4}$). 2. Simulated relative frequency $= \frac{64}{160} = \frac{2}{5} = 0.40$, which is $0.40 - 0.375 = 0.025$ above the theoretical value.

**Final Answer**: (a) $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$, (b) Simulated RF $= \frac{64}{160} = 0.40$, which is $0.025$ ($\frac{1}{40}$) higher than the theoretical probability $0.375$
</details>

#### Question `9_2_Q8` [Challenging] *(Two-Step Simulation & Complements)*
A multiple-choice quiz has $2$ questions, each with $5$ options (A, B, C, D, E) and only $1$ correct answer. A student guesses completely at random on both questions. (a) How would you assign integers in `RANDBETWEEN(1, 5)` to simulate guessing one question? (b) Find the theoretical probability of guessing **at least one** question correctly out of the $2$ questions.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $P(\text{Correct}) = \frac{1}{5}$, assign $1$ to Correct and $2, 3, 4, 5$ to Incorrect in `RANDBETWEEN(1, 5)`.
2. The probability of getting **both wrong** is $\frac{4}{5} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{16}{25}$. Therefore, $P(\text{at least one correct}) = 1 - \frac{16}{25} = \frac{9}{25} = 0.36$.

**Final Answer**: (a) Assign $1 =$ Correct ($P = \frac{1}{5}$) and $2, 3, 4, 5 =$ Incorrect ($P = \frac{4}{5}$); (b) $\frac{9}{25} = 0.36$
</details>

#### Question `9_2_Q9` [Medium] *(Statistical Inference from Relative Frequency)*
A six-sided die is suspected of being weighted towards $6$. In $300$ rolls, the number $6$ appears $96$ times. Calculate the expected number of $6$s for a fair die and the experimental relative frequency, and justify whether the evidence suggests the die is biased.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. For a fair die, $P(6) = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$, so in $300$ rolls the expected number of $6$s is $300 \times \frac{1}{6} = 50$.
2. The observed frequency of $96$ gives a relative frequency of $\frac{96}{300} = 0.32$ — almost twice the theoretical probability across a large sample of $300$ trials, which is strong evidence of bias.

**Final Answer**: Expected $= 50$, Observed $= 96$ ($\text{RF} = 0.32$ vs theoretical $0.167$). Yes, rolling $96$ sixes in $300$ trials is nearly double the expected count of $50$, providing strong evidence that the die is biased towards $6$.
</details>

#### Question `9_2_Q10` [Challenging] *(Sample Size & Variability)*
Two hospitals in Victoria record the proportion of baby boys born (where the long-run probability is $P(\text{Boy}) \approx 0.5$). Small Hospital A averages $10$ births per week, while Large Hospital B averages $100$ births per week. Which hospital is **more likely** to record a week where $70\%$ or more of the babies born are boys? Explain your reasoning using the Law of Large Numbers.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

**Final Answer**: Small Hospital A ($10$ births/week) is much more likely to have a week with $\ge 70\%$ boys because small samples have much higher random variability around $0.5$, whereas in $100$ births at Hospital B the Law of Large Numbers keeps the relative frequency tightly clustered near $0.50$.

#### Question `9_2_Q11` [Challenging] *(Critiquing Digital Simulations)*
Noah wants to simulate the sum of two rolled six-sided dice in a spreadsheet. He writes `=RANDBETWEEN(2, 12)` instead of `=RANDBETWEEN(1, 6) + RANDBETWEEN(1, 6)`. Explain mathematically why Noah's single-command formula is an invalid simulation of two dice, comparing the probability of rolling a sum of $7$ with a sum of $2$ in both methods.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Noah's formula `RANDBETWEEN(2, 12)` assigns an equal probability of $\frac{1}{11} \approx 0.091$ to every sum from $2$ to $12$.
2. When two real dice are rolled (`RANDBETWEEN(1, 6) + RANDBETWEEN(1, 6)`), there is only $1$ way to make $2$ ($(1,1) \implies P = \frac{1}{36} \approx 0.028$) but $6$ ways to make $7$ ($P = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.167$).

**Final Answer**: `RANDBETWEEN(2, 12)` makes all $11$ sums ($2$ to $12$) equally likely with probability $\frac{1}{11}$ each. In reality, two dice have $36$ equally likely $(d_1, d_2)$ outcomes where $P(\text{sum}=2) = \frac{1}{36}$ and $P(\text{sum}=7) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}$.
</details>
