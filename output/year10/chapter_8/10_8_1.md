### Key Vocabulary
- **Conditional probability ($\Pr(A \mid B)$)**: The probability that event $A$ occurs given that event $B$ is already known to have occurred, restricting the sample space to $B$: $\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(B)} = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)}$ (for $\Pr(B) > 0$).
- **Independent events**: Two events $A$ and $B$ whose occurrence has no effect on each other's probability, satisfying $\Pr(A \mid B) = \Pr(A)$ and the multiplication test $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A) \times \Pr(B)$.
- **Mutually exclusive (disjoint) events**: Two events that cannot occur at the same time ($A \cap B = \varnothing$, so $\Pr(A \cap B) = 0$). Two events with non-zero probabilities cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent.
- **Sampling without replacement and factorials ($n!$)**: Selecting items sequentially without returning them to the pool, so conditional branch probabilities change at each stage; the number of ways to arrange $n$ distinct items in a row is $n! = n(n - 1)(n - 2)\cdots 1$.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Conditional Probability and Restricted Sample Spaces

Phrases such as 'given that $B$', 'if $B$', 'knowing that $B$', or 'of those in $B$, find the probability of $A$' all describe conditional probability, denoted $\Pr(A \mid B)$. Knowing that $B$ has occurred shrinks the sample space from the universal set $\xi$ down to $B$ alone, and the favourable outcomes are only those inside the intersection $A \cap B$. Rearranging the formula produces the general Multiplication Law of Probability: $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(B) \times \Pr(A \mid B)$.

$$\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(B)} = \frac{n(A \cap B)}{n(B)}, \qquad \Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(B)\Pr(A \mid B)$$

> **💡 What Follows 'GIVEN' Goes in the Denominator**
> In $\Pr(A \mid B)$, the condition $B$ ('given $B$' or 'of the $B$s') ALWAYS forms the denominator $\Pr(B)$, while the intersection $\Pr(A \cap B)$ ALWAYS forms the numerator.

### Testing for Independence vs Mutual Exclusivity

Events $A$ and $B$ are mathematically independent if knowing that $B$ occurred does not change the probability of $A$, i.e. $\Pr(A \mid B) = \Pr(A)$. Substituting this into the conditional probability formula gives the universal test for independence: $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A) \times \Pr(B)$. Be careful never to confuse independent events with mutually exclusive (disjoint) events: if $A$ and $B$ have positive probabilities and are mutually exclusive ($\Pr(A \cap B) = 0$), knowing $B$ happened guarantees $A$ did NOT happen ($\Pr(A \mid B) = 0 \neq \Pr(A)$), so mutually exclusive events are strongly dependent!

$$A \text{ and } B \text{ are independent} \iff \Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A) \times \Pr(B) \iff \Pr(A \mid B) = \Pr(A)$$

> **💡 Compare $\Pr(A \cap B)$ Against $\Pr(A) \times \Pr(B)$**
> To prove or disprove independence, calculate $\Pr(A \cap B)$ and $\Pr(A) \times \Pr(B)$ separately. If they are equal, $A$ and $B$ are independent; if not, they are dependent.

### Multi-Stage Tree Diagrams Without Replacement and Counting Principles (10A)

When items are drawn from a finite group with replacement, the stage probabilities stay constant and the draws are independent. When items are drawn without replacement, both the total denominator and the remaining counts decrease by $1$ after each draw, so the second-stage branch probabilities are conditional probabilities $\Pr(B \mid A)$. In Year 10A combinatorics (VC2M10AP02), by the multiplication principle, if step 1 can be done in $m$ ways and step 2 in $n$ ways, there are $m \times n$ outcomes; $n$ distinct objects can be arranged in $n!$ ways, and a subset of $r$ items can be chosen from $n$ items in $\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!}$ ways.

$$n! = n(n - 1)\cdots 1, \qquad \binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!}$$

> **💡 Reduce Both Numerator and Denominator Without Replacement**
> When drawing a second item of the same colour without replacement from $k$ favourable out of $N$ total items, the conditional probability is $\frac{k - 1}{N - 1}$, not $\frac{k}{N}$.

### WE10_8_1_1: Conditional Probability from a Two-Way Table (Karnaugh Map)

**Problem**: A survey of $100$ Year 10 students in Melbourne asked whether they play Basketball ($B$) and/or Tennis ($T$). In total, $50$ play Basketball, $40$ play Tennis, and $20$ play both sports. Find: (a) $\Pr(B \cup T)$, (b) $\Pr(B \mid T)$ (the probability a student plays Basketball given they play Tennis), and (c) $\Pr(T \mid B)$, and determine whether $B$ and $T$ are independent.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Use the Addition Rule $\Pr(B \cup T) = \Pr(B) + \Pr(T) - \Pr(B \cap T)$ with $\Pr(B) = \frac{50}{100} = \frac{1}{2}$, $\Pr(T) = \frac{40}{100} = \frac{2}{5}$, and $\Pr(B \cap T) = \frac{20}{100} = \frac{1}{5}$. | $$\Pr(B \cup T) = \frac{50}{100} + \frac{40}{100} - \frac{20}{100} = \frac{70}{100} = \frac{7}{10}$$ |
| 2 | Evaluate $\Pr(B \mid T) = \frac{n(B \cap T)}{n(T)}$ and $\Pr(T \mid B) = \frac{n(B \cap T)}{n(B)}$. | $$\Pr(B \mid T) = \frac{20}{40} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \Pr(T \mid B) = \frac{20}{50} = \frac{2}{5}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\frac{7}{10}$, (b) $\frac{1}{2}$, (c) $\frac{2}{5}$; $B$ and $T$ are independent because $\Pr(B \cap T) = \Pr(B)\Pr(T) = \frac{1}{5}$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Confusing $\Pr(A \mid B)$ with $\Pr(B \mid A)$ ( the Prosecutor's / Inverse Fallacy)
- **The Misconception**: Assuming that 'the probability of $A$ given $B$' equals 'the probability of $B$ given $A$' (e.g. confusing the probability of having a disease given a positive test with the probability of a positive test given the disease).
- **The Correct Concept**: $\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(B)}$ and $\Pr(B \mid A) = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(A)}$ share the same numerator $\Pr(A \cap B)$ but divide by completely different denominators! They are equal only when $\Pr(A) = \Pr(B)$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\Pr(A \mid B) = \Pr(B \mid A) \quad \text{(Incorrect whenever } \Pr(A) \neq \Pr(B)\text{)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\Pr(A \mid B) = \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(B)} \neq \frac{\Pr(A \cap B)}{\Pr(A)} = \Pr(B \mid A)$$
- **Counter-Example**: Given a person plays in the AFL ($B$), the probability they live in Australia ($A$) is nearly $1$ ($\Pr(A \mid B) \approx 1$), whereas given a person lives in Australia ($A$), the probability they play in the AFL ($B$) is tiny ($\Pr(B \mid A) < 0.0001$).

### Thinking Mutually Exclusive Events Are Independent
- **The Misconception**: Believing that because two disjoint events $A$ and $B$ have 'nothing in common' ($A \cap B = \varnothing$), they must be independent.
- **The Correct Concept**: Mutually exclusive events with $\Pr(A) > 0$ and $\Pr(B) > 0$ are maximally DEPENDENT: if $B$ happens, $A$ is completely blocked from happening ($\Pr(A \mid B) = 0 \neq \Pr(A)$). For independent events, the overlap must be non-zero: $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A)\Pr(B) > 0$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$A \cap B = \varnothing \implies A \text{ and } B \text{ are independent} \quad \text{(False!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$A \cap B = \varnothing \implies \Pr(A \cap B) = 0 \neq \Pr(A)\Pr(B) \implies A \text{ and } B \text{ are dependent}$$
- **Counter-Example**: On a single roll of a die, rolling a $6$ ($A$) and rolling an odd number ($B$) are mutually exclusive: if you are told the roll was odd, the probability it is a $6$ drops from $\frac{1}{6}$ to $0$.

Given $\Pr(A) = \frac{1}{2}$, $\Pr(B) = \frac{2}{5}$, and $\Pr(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{5}$, find (a) $\Pr(A \cup B)$, (b) $\Pr(A \mid B)$, (c) $\Pr(B \mid A)$, and (d) state whether $A$ and $B$ are independent.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

A fair $6$-sided die is rolled once. Let event $E$ be 'rolling an even number' ($\{2, 4, 6\}$) and event $G$ be 'rolling a number greater than $3$' ($\{4, 5, 6\}$). Find (a) $\Pr(E \cap G)$, (b) $\Pr(E \mid G)$, and (c) determine whether $E$ and $G$ are independent.

#### Question `10_8_1_Q3` [Medium] *(Understanding)*
Two events $A$ and $B$ are known to be independent with $\Pr(A) = 0.4$ and $\Pr(B) = 0.3$. Find (a) $\Pr(A \cap B)$, (b) $\Pr(A \cup B)$, and (c) $\Pr(A \mid B)$.

#### Question `10_8_1_Q6` [Challenging] *(Reliability Engineering)*
A solar battery backup system has two independent power inverters $I_1$ and $I_2$. On any given storm day, the probability that $I_1$ functions without fault is $0.90$ and the probability that $I_2$ functions without fault is $0.80$. #### Question `10_8_1_Q7` [Challenging] *(Tree Diagrams & Bayes' Rule)*

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `10_8_1_Q9` [Challenging] *(Medical Screening & Base-Rate Fallacy)*
A medical screening test for a rare condition ($1\%$ prevalence in the population, so $\Pr(D) = 0.01$ and $\Pr(D') = 0.99$) correctly tests positive ($+$) for $99\%$ of people with the condition ($\Pr(+ \mid D) = 0.99$), but also gives a false positive for $1\%$ of healthy people ($\Pr(+ \mid D') = 0.01$). Show that if a randomly tested person receives a positive result, the actual probability $\Pr(D \mid +)$ that they have the condition is only $\frac{1}{2}$ ($50\%$), and explain why people often mistakenly think it is $99\%$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Calculate $\Pr(D \cap +) = 0.01 \times 0.99 = 0.0099$ (true positives) and $\Pr(D' \cap +) = 0.99 \times 0.01 = 0.0099$ (false positives), so $\Pr(+) = 0.0099 + 0.0099 = 0.0198$.
2. Therefore $\Pr(D \mid +) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0198} = \frac{1}{2} = 50\%$. Because the condition is rare ($1\%$), the number of false positives from the large healthy group equals the number of true positives.

**Final Answer**: $\Pr(+) = 0.01(0.99) + 0.99(0.01) = 0.0198 \implies \Pr(D \mid +) = \frac{0.0099}{0.0198} = \frac{1}{2} = 50\%$ (people confuse $\Pr(D \mid +)$ with $\Pr(+ \mid D) = 99\%$)
</details>

#### Question `10_8_1_Q10` [Challenging] *(Algebraic Proof of Complement Independence)*
Prove algebraically that if $A$ and $B$ are independent events (so $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A)\Pr(B)$) with $\Pr(B') > 0$, then $A$ and $B'$ (the complement of $B$) are also independent events.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Partition event $A$ into two disjoint parts: $A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B')$, so $\Pr(A \cap B') = \Pr(A) - \Pr(A \cap B)$.
2. Substitute $\Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A)\Pr(B)$ and factorise: $\Pr(A \cap B') = \Pr(A)(1 - \Pr(B)) = \Pr(A)\Pr(B')$, which proves $A$ and $B'$ are independent.

**Final Answer**: $\Pr(A \cap B') = \Pr(A) - \Pr(A \cap B) = \Pr(A) - \Pr(A)\Pr(B) = \Pr(A)(1 - \Pr(B)) = \Pr(A)\Pr(B')$
</details>

#### Question `10_8_1_Q11` [Extension] *(Three-Stage Sample Space)*
A fair coin is tossed $3$ times. Let event $A$ be 'the first toss is Heads', event $B$ be 'the second toss is Heads', and event $C$ be 'exactly $2$ of the $3$ tosses are Heads'. Show whether $A$ and $C$ are independent.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Of the $8$ equally likely outcomes, $C = \{\text{HHT, HTH, THH}\}$ has $\Pr(C) = \frac{3}{8}$, and $A \cap C = \{\text{HHT, HTH}\}$ has $\Pr(A \cap C) = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4}$.
2. Since $\Pr(A)\Pr(C) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{3}{16} \neq \frac{1}{4}$, events $A$ and $C$ are dependent ($\Pr(C \mid A) = \frac{1}{2} > \frac{3}{8}$).

**Final Answer**: Not independent: $\Pr(A) = \frac{1}{2}$, $\Pr(C) = \frac{3}{8}$, and $\Pr(A \cap C) = \frac{2}{8} = \frac{1}{4} \neq \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{3}{16}$
</details>

#### Question `10_8_1_Q12` [Extension] *(The Monty Hall Conditional Probability Paradox)*
[10A] The Monty Hall problem: A contestant chooses Door 1 out of $3$ doors (behind $1$ door is a car with probability $\frac{1}{3}$; behind the other $2$ doors are goats). The host, who knows where the car is, opens one of the other two doors (say Door 3) to reveal a goat. By tracing a tree diagram based on the car's initial location (Door 1, Door 2, or Door 3, each $\frac{1}{3}$), prove that switching to the remaining closed door wins the car with probability $\frac{2}{3}$, whereas staying with Door 1 wins with probability $\frac{1}{3}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. If the car is behind Door 1 (probability $\frac{1}{3}$), switching loses and staying wins.
2. If the car is behind Door 2 (probability $\frac{1}{3}$) or Door 3 (probability $\frac{1}{3}$), the host is forced to open the only other goat door, leaving the car behind the unopened switch door with total probability $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$.

**Final Answer**: Staying wins if and only if the car was initially behind Door 1 ($\Pr = \frac{1}{3}$); switching wins whenever the car was initially behind Door 2 or Door 3 ($\Pr = \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3}$)
</details>
