### Key Vocabulary
- **Deductive proof**: A step-by-step logical chain of mathematical statements, each justified by an established definition, axiom, or theorem (such as congruence tests SSS, SAS, ASA, RHS or parallel line properties), leading irrefutably from given premises to a conclusion.
- **Graph (network), vertices ($V$), edges ($E$), and faces ($F$)**: A mathematical structure consisting of vertices (nodes) connected by edges (links); for any connected planar graph (drawn without edge crossings), Euler's formula states $V - E + F = 2$ (including the infinite outer face).
- **Vertex degree ($\deg(v)$) and Eulerian trail/circuit**: The degree of a vertex is the number of edges incident to it (where $\sum \deg(v) = 2E$). A connected network has an Eulerian circuit (traversing every edge once and returning to the start) if and only if every vertex has even degree, and an open Eulerian trail if and only if it has exactly $2$ vertices of odd degree.
- **Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) and shortest path**: A spanning tree of a connected graph on $V$ vertices is a cycle-free subgraph connecting all $V$ vertices using exactly $V - 1$ edges; a Minimum Spanning Tree has the smallest possible total edge weight, found via Kruskal's or Prim's greedy algorithm.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Formal Deductive Proofs Using Triangle Congruence and Similarity

In deductive geometry, visual appearance is never accepted as proof. To prove properties of triangles and quadrilaterals (for example, that the opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal, or that the diagonals of a rhombus bisect each other at $90^\circ$), split the figure into triangles and prove they are congruent using one of the four congruence tests: SSS, SAS, ASA (or AAS), or RHS. Once $\triangle ABC \equiv \triangle DEF$ is established, any pair of matching sides or angles is equal by the reason 'matching sides/angles of congruent triangles (CPCTC)'.

$$\text{Congruence tests: } \text{SSS}, \quad \text{SAS}, \quad \text{ASA}, \quad \text{RHS} \implies \text{matching parts equal}$$

> **💡 Three Equal Pairs + Test Name + CPCTC**
> Every congruence proof follows a $5$-line structure: (1)–(3) state three pairs of equal sides/angles with bracketed geometric reasons, (4) state $\triangle_1 \equiv \triangle_2$ with the test code, and (5) deduce the required matching side or angle.

### Planar Networks, the Handshaking Lemma, and Eulerian Trails

A network (graph) models connections between objects. Since each edge has two endpoints, the sum of the degrees of all vertices always equals twice the number of edges ($\sum \deg(v) = 2E$, the Handshaking Lemma). For any connected planar graph drawn without intersecting edges, Euler's polyhedral/planar formula relates the number of vertices $V$, edges $E$, and regions/faces $F$ (counting the exterior region as $1$ face): $V - E + F = 2$. Euler's Konigsberg Bridge theorem tells us immediately whether every edge can be traversed exactly once: $0$ odd-degree vertices gives an Eulerian circuit, $2$ odd-degree vertices gives an Eulerian trail starting at one odd vertex and ending at the other, and more than $2$ odd vertices makes an Eulerian traversal impossible.

$$\sum_{v \in V} \deg(v) = 2E, \qquad V - E + F = 2 \quad \text{(connected planar graph)}$$

> **💡 Count the Odd-Degree Vertices**
> $0$ odd vertices $\implies$ Eulerian circuit (start and end at same vertex). $2$ odd vertices $\implies$ Eulerian trail (start at one odd vertex, finish at the other). $> 2$ odd vertices $\implies$ cannot traverse every edge once.

### Network Algorithms: Shortest Paths and Minimum Spanning Trees

In a weighted network where edge weights represent distances, travel times, or cabling costs, two classic optimisation problems arise. (1) The Shortest Path Problem (solved by Dijkstra's algorithm) finds the path of minimum total weight between a start vertex $S$ and a destination vertex $T$ by iteratively locking in the unvisited vertex with the smallest cumulative distance from $S$. (2) The Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) Problem (solved by Kruskal's algorithm or Prim's algorithm) connects all $V$ vertices together using $V - 1$ edges of minimum total weight without forming any closed cycles.

$$E_{\text{spanning tree}} = V - 1, \qquad W_{\text{MST}} = \min \sum_{e \in T} w(e)$$

> **💡 Kruskal's Algorithm: Sort Edges Shortest-First and Reject Cycles**
> To build an MST by Kruskal's algorithm, list all edges in increasing order of weight and add them one by one, skipping any edge that would complete a closed loop, until $V - 1$ edges are chosen.

**Problem**: Let $ABCD$ be a parallelogram with $AB \parallel DC$ and $AD \parallel BC$. By drawing the diagonal $AC$, write a formal deductive proof that $AB = DC$ and $AD = BC$, and calculate the perimeter of $ABCD$ if $AB = 3x + 4\text{ cm}$, $DC = 5x - 6\text{ cm}$, and $BC = 2x + 1\text{ cm}$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle CDA$, use alternate interior angles on parallel lines $AB \parallel DC$ and $AD \parallel BC$ with transversal $AC$, plus the common side $AC$. | $$\angle BAC = \angle DCA \quad (\text{alternate angles, } AB \parallel DC)$$
$$\angle BCA = \angle DAC \quad (\text{alternate angles, } AD \parallel BC)$$
$$AC = CA \quad (\text{common side})$$ |
| 2 | Conclude congruence by ASA and equate matching opposite sides. | $$\therefore \triangle ABC \equiv \triangle CDA \quad (\text{ASA}) \implies AB = CD \text{ and } BC = DA \quad (\text{matching sides of congruent triangles})$$ |
| 3 | Set $AB = DC$ to solve for $x$, then evaluate all four side lengths to find the perimeter. | $$3x + 4 = 5x - 6 \implies 2x = 10 \implies x = 5$$
$$AB = DC = 19\text{ cm}, \quad BC = AD = 2(5) + 1 = 11\text{ cm} \implies \text{Perimeter} = 2(19 + 11) = 60\text{ cm}$$ |

**Answer**: Proof by ASA ($\triangle ABC \equiv \triangle CDA$); $x = 5$ and $\text{Perimeter} = 60\text{ cm}$

### WE10_7_1_3: Shortest Path and Minimum Spanning Tree on a Weighted Network

**Problem**: Five regional towns $A, B, C, D, E$ in Victoria are to be linked by optical fibre along existing road reserves with distances (in $\text{km}$): $AB = 4$, $AC = 8$, $BC = 3$, $BD = 6$, $CD = 4$, $CE = 7$, and $DE = 5$. Find: (a) the shortest path distance from town $A$ to town $E$, and (b) the minimum total length of optical fibre required to connect all $5$ towns in a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST).

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | For the shortest path from $A$ to $E$, apply Dijkstra's algorithm by finding the minimum cumulative distance from $A$ to each vertex in order: $d(A) = 0$, $d(B) = 4$ (via $AB$), $d(C) = \min(8,\, 4 + 3) = 7$ (via $A\text{–}B\text{–}C$), $d(D) = \min(4 + 6,\, 7 + 4) = 10$ (via $A\text{–}B\text{–}D$), and $d(E) = \min(7 + 7,\, 10 + 5) = 14$ (via $A\text{–}B\text{–}C\text{–}E$). | $$\text{Shortest path } A \to B \to C \to E = 4 + 3 + 7 = 14\text{ km}$$ |
| 2 | For the MST on $V = 5$ vertices, we need $V - 1 = 4$ edges. Sort all edges by weight: $BC(3), AB(4), CD(4), DE(5), BD(6), CE(7), AC(8)$ and apply Kruskal's algorithm. | $$\text{Select } BC(3), \text{ then } AB(4), \text{ then } CD(4), \text{ then } DE(5) \quad (\text{no cycles formed, } 4 \text{ edges selected})$$
$$W_{\text{MST}} = 3 + 4 + 4 + 5 = 16\text{ km}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Shortest path $A \to B \to C \to E$ has length $14\text{ km}$; (b) Minimum Spanning Tree has total length $16\text{ km}$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Using 'SSA' or 'AAA' as a Triangle Congruence Test
- **The Misconception**: Claiming two triangles are congruent because they have three equal angles (AAA) or two equal sides and a non-included angle (SSA).
- **The Correct Concept**: AAA proves similarity (same shape, possibly different scale factor), never congruence. SSA is the ambiguous case (unless the angle is $90^\circ$, which is the valid RHS test). For SAS, the equal angle MUST be the included angle sandwiched between the two equal sides.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\triangle ABC \equiv \triangle DEF \quad (\text{AAA or SSA}) \quad \text{(Invalid congruence reason!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Valid congruence tests: } \text{SSS}, \quad \text{SAS (included angle)}, \quad \text{ASA / AAS}, \quad \text{RHS}$$
- **Counter-Example**: Two equilateral triangles of side $3\text{ cm}$ and side $10\text{ cm}$ share all three angles ($60^\circ, 60^\circ, 60^\circ$) but are clearly not congruent.

### Forgetting the Infinite Exterior Face in Euler's Formula $V - E + F = 2$
- **The Misconception**: Counting only the bounded enclosed polygons inside a planar network and getting $V - E + F = 1$.
- **The Correct Concept**: In Euler's planar formula $V - E + F = 2$, the unbounded region surrounding the outside of the graph ALWAYS counts as $1$ face. For example, a single triangle has $V = 3$ vertices, $E = 3$ edges, and $F = 2$ faces ($1$ interior + $1$ exterior): $3 - 3 + 2 = 2$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\text{Single triangle: } V = 3,\, E = 3,\, F = 1 \implies V - E + F = 1 \quad \text{(Forgot exterior face!)}$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\text{Single triangle: } V = 3,\, E = 3,\, F = 2 \text{ (1 inside + 1 outside)} \implies 3 - 3 + 2 = 2$$
- **Counter-Example**: A tree network with no closed loops has $0$ enclosed regions, so $F = 1$ (the outside face alone), matching $V - (V - 1) + 1 = 2$.

#### Question `10_7_1_Q9` [Challenging] *(Number Theory in Graph Theory)*
Use the Handshaking Lemma $\sum \deg(v) = 2E$ to prove that in ANY network, the number of vertices with an odd degree must always be an even number (i.e. it is impossible to draw a graph with an odd number of odd-degree vertices).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Split the sum of all vertex degrees into even-degree vertices and odd-degree vertices: $\sum_{\text{even}} \deg(v) + \sum_{\text{odd}} \deg(v) = 2E$.
2. Because $2E$ is even and $\sum_{\text{even}} \deg(v)$ is even, their difference $\sum_{\text{odd}} \deg(v)$ must be even; since a sum of odd integers is even if and only if there are an even number of terms, the count of odd-degree vertices is always even.

**Final Answer**: Since $\sum \deg(v) = 2E$ is even and the sum of even degrees is even, the sum of the odd degrees must also be even, which requires an even number of odd summands
</details>

Prove deductively that the diagonals of a rhombus $ABCD$ (where $AB = BC = CD = DA$) intersect at right angles ($90^\circ$), given that the diagonals of any parallelogram bisect each other at $M$ ($AM = CM$ and $BM = DM$).

#### Question `10_7_1_Q12` [Extension] *(Polyhedral Topology)*
A soccer ball (truncated icosahedron) is a connected planar polyhedron made of $12$ regular pentagons and $20$ regular hexagons ($F = 32$ faces in total), where every vertex has degree $3$. Use the face-edge relation and Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ to find the exact number of edges $E$ and vertices $V$ on a soccer ball.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

**Final Answer**: $E = 90$ edges and $V = 60$ vertices
</details>
