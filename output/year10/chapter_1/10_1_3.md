### Key Vocabulary
- **Fractional index**: A rational exponent $\frac{m}{n}$ where the denominator $n$ indicates the $n$-th root and the numerator $m$ indicates the power: $a^{m/n} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m$.
- **Logarithm**: For $a > 0, a \neq 1$ and $y > 0$, the logarithm $\log_a(y)$ is the exponent $x$ to which the base $a$ must be raised to produce $y$: $\log_a(y) = x \iff a^x = y$.
- **Logarithm laws**: Rules derived from the index laws that convert products into sums ($\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y$), quotients into differences ($\log_a(x/y) = \log_a x - \log_a y$), and powers into multiples ($\log_a(x^p) = p\log_a x$).
- **Logarithmic scale**: A measurement scale (such as the Richter scale for earthquake magnitude or decibels for sound intensity) where each unit increase represents a tenfold multiplication of the underlying physical quantity.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Fractional Indices and $n$-th Roots

By the third index law $(a^p)^q = a^{pq}$, we have $(a^{1/n})^n = a^1 = a$, which defines $a^{1/n}$ as the principal $n$-th root $\sqrt[n]{a}$ (for $a > 0$). More generally, for a rational exponent $\frac{m}{n}$, raising $a$ to the power $\frac{m}{n}$ means taking the $n$-th root and raising the result to the power $m$. Evaluating the $n$-th root FIRST keeps the intermediate numbers small and easy to compute without a calculator.

$$a^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{a}, \quad a^{m/n} = (\sqrt[n]{a})^m = \sqrt[n]{a^m}, \quad a^{-m/n} = \frac{1}{(\sqrt[n]{a})^m}$$

> **💡 Root the Denominator First, Then Raise to the Numerator**
> To evaluate $27^{2/3}$ mentally, take the cube root first ($\sqrt[3]{27} = 3$) and then square it ($3^2 = 9$), rather than computing $\sqrt[3]{27^2} = \sqrt[3]{729}$.

### Definition of a Logarithm

A logarithm answers the question: 'To what power must I raise the base $a$ to obtain $y$?' For any base $a > 0$ ($a \neq 1$) and any positive number $y > 0$, the statement $\log_a(y) = x$ is completely equivalent to the index statement $a^x = y$. Because $a^0 = 1$ and $a^1 = a$, it immediately follows that $\log_a(1) = 0$ and $\log_a(a) = 1$ for every valid base $a$.

$$\log_a(y) = x \iff a^x = y \quad (a > 0,\; a \neq 1,\; y > 0)$$

> **💡 A Logarithm IS an Exponent**
> Read $\log_2(8) = 3$ as 'the power to which $2$ is raised to give $8$ is $3$' ($2^3 = 8$). If the argument is a fraction, use negative indices: $\log_3\left(\frac{1}{9}\right) = \log_3(3^{-2}) = -2$.

### The Laws of Logarithms and Logarithmic Scales

Because logarithms are exponents, the index laws $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$, $a^m \div a^n = a^{m-n}$, and $(a^m)^p = a^{mp}$ translate directly into three fundamental logarithm laws for any positive $x, y > 0$. Base-$10$ (common) logarithms are used in science to compress vast ranges of magnitudes onto human-readable scales, such as sound level in decibels $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$ and pH $= -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$.

$$\log_a(xy) = \log_a x + \log_a y, \quad \log_a\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) = \log_a x - \log_a y, \quad \log_a(x^p) = p\log_a x$$

> **💡 Log of a Product is the Sum of the Logs**
> Combine sums of logs with the same base by multiplying their arguments: $\log_6(4) + \log_6(9) = \log_6(4 \times 9) = \log_6(36) = 2$.

**Problem**: Evaluate without a calculator: (a) $16^{3/4}$ and (b) $27^{-2/3}$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), take the $4$-th root of $16$ first ($\sqrt[4]{16} = 2$) and then raise to the power $3$. | $$16^{3/4} = (\sqrt[4]{16})^3 = 2^3 = 8$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $8$, (b) $\frac{1}{9}$

### WE10_1_3_3: Simplifying Expressions Using Logarithm Laws

**Problem**: Simplify and evaluate $2\log_3(6) - \log_3(4)$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Apply the power law $p\log_a(x) = \log_a(x^p)$ to the first term. | $$2\log_3(6) - \log_3(4) = \log_3(6^2) - \log_3(4) = \log_3(36) - \log_3(4)$$ |
| 2 | Apply the quotient law $\log_a(x) - \log_a(y) = \log_a(x/y)$ and evaluate. | $$= \log_3\!\left(\frac{36}{4}\right) = \log_3(9) = \log_3(3^2) = 2$$ |

**Answer**: $2$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Confusing a Negative Exponent with a Negative Number
- **The Misconception**: Writing $8^{-2/3} = -4$ instead of $\frac{1}{4}$.
- **The Correct Concept**: A negative index indicates the reciprocal, NOT a negative sign: $a^{-p} = \frac{1}{a^p}$. Thus $8^{-2/3} = \frac{1}{8^{2/3}} = \frac{1}{4}$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$8^{-2/3} = -(\sqrt[3]{8})^2 = -4 \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$8^{-2/3} = \frac{1}{(\sqrt[3]{8})^2} = \frac{1}{2^2} = \frac{1}{4} \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: A positive base raised to any real exponent is always strictly positive ($8^x > 0$ for all real $x$).

### Misapplying the Product Law to a Sum Inside a Logarithm
- **The Misconception**: Writing $\log_a(x + y) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$ or $\frac{\log_a(x)}{\log_a(y)} = \log_a(x - y)$.
- **The Correct Concept**: The sum of two logarithms is the logarithm of their PRODUCT: $\log_a(x) + \log_a(y) = \log_a(xy)$. There is no expansion formula for $\log_a(x + y)$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\log_2(4 + 4) = \log_2(4) + \log_2(4) = 2 + 2 = 4 \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\log_2(4 + 4) = \log_2(8) = 3, \quad \text{and} \quad \log_2(4) + \log_2(4) = \log_2(4 \times 4) = \log_2(16) = 4 \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: For $x = 4, y = 4$ and base $2$: $\log_2(4 + 4) = \log_2(8) = 3$, whereas $\log_2(4) + \log_2(4) = 2 + 2 = 4 \neq 3$.

#### Question `10_1_3_Q3` [Easy] *(Evaluating Logarithms)*
Evaluate $\log_{10}(1000) + \log_4\!\left(\frac{1}{16}\right)$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $1000 = 10^3$, $\log_{10}(1000) = 3$. Since $\frac{1}{16} = 4^{-2}$, $\log_4\!\left(\frac{1}{16}\right) = -2$.
2. Add the results: $3 + (-2) = 1$.

**Final Answer**: $1$
</details>

**Final Answer**: $2$

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `10_1_3_Q5` [Challenging] *(Solving Logarithmic Equations)*
Solve for $x$: $\log_2(x - 3) + \log_2(x + 3) = 4$, where $x > 3$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Combine the logarithms using the product law: $\log_2((x - 3)(x + 3)) = 4 \implies \log_2(x^2 - 9) = 4$.
2. Convert to index form: $x^2 - 9 = 2^4 = 16 \implies x^2 = 25$.
3. Since $x > 3$, take the positive root $x = 5$.

**Final Answer**: $x = 5$
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q6` [Medium] *(Richter Scale Comparison)*
On the Richter logarithmic scale, the magnitude $M$ of an earthquake of intensity $I$ is $M = \log_{10}(I/I_0)$. How many times more intense is a magnitude $7$ earthquake than a magnitude $4$ earthquake?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. From $M_1 - M_2 = \log_{10}(I_1 / I_2)$, we have $7 - 4 = 3 = \log_{10}(I_1 / I_2)$.
2. Converting to index form gives $\frac{I_1}{I_2} = 10^3 = 1000$.

**Final Answer**: $1000$ times more intense
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q7` [Medium] *(Decibel Sound Scale)*
Sound loudness $L$ in decibels (dB) is modelled by $L = 10\log_{10}(I/I_0)$, where $I_0 = 10^{-12}\text{ W/m}^2$. Find the loudness in decibels of a Melbourne tram bell whose sound intensity is $I = 10^{-3}\text{ W/m}^2$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Compute the intensity ratio: $\frac{I}{I_0} = \frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-12}} = 10^9$.
2. Substitute into the decibel formula: $L = 10\log_{10}(10^9) = 10 \times 9 = 90\text{ dB}$.

**Final Answer**: $90\text{ dB}$
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q8` [Medium] *(Exponential Growth Time)*
A bacterial culture doubles every hour according to $N = 500 \times 2^t$. Use logarithms to find the exact number of hours $t$ required for the population to reach $16\,000$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Substitute $N = 16\,000$: $500 \times 2^t = 16\,000 \implies 2^t = \frac{16\,000}{500} = 32$.
2. Using logarithms, $t = \log_2(32) = 5\text{ hours}$.

**Final Answer**: $5\text{ hours}$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `10_1_3_Q9` [Challenging] *(Proof of First Logarithm Law)*
Use the index law $a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$ to prove the logarithm product law $\log_a(xy) = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$ for $x, y > 0$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Let $m = \log_a(x)$ and $n = \log_a(y)$. By the definition of a logarithm, $x = a^m$ and $y = a^n$.
2. Multiply $x$ and $y$: $xy = a^m \times a^n = a^{m+n}$.
3. Rewrite $xy = a^{m+n}$ in logarithmic form: $\log_a(xy) = m + n = \log_a(x) + \log_a(y)$.

**Final Answer**: Let $m = \log_a x$ and $n = \log_a y$, so $x = a^m, y = a^n \implies xy = a^{m+n} \implies \log_a(xy) = m + n$
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q10` [Medium] *(Domain of the Logarithmic Function)*
Explain why $\log_a(y)$ is undefined in the real number system when $y \le 0$ (given $a > 0, a \neq 1$).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By definition, $\log_a(y) = x$ means $a^x = y$.
2. Because the base $a$ is strictly positive, raising $a$ to any real power $x$ always produces a strictly positive number ($a^x > 0$). Therefore, no real exponent $x$ can make $a^x \le 0$.

**Final Answer**: Since $a > 0$, $a^x > 0$ for every real exponent $x$, so $a^x$ can never equal $0$ or a negative number
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q11` [Challenging] *(Change-of-Base Ratio)*
Simplify $\frac{\log_a(27)}{\log_a(3)}$ and explain why the result does not depend on the base $a$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Write $27 = 3^3$ and apply the power law to the numerator: $\log_a(27) = \log_a(3^3) = 3\log_a(3)$.
2. Divide by $\log_a(3)$: $\frac{3\log_a(3)}{\log_a(3)} = 3$, since $\log_a(3) \neq 0$ cancels out completely.

**Final Answer**: $3$
</details>

#### Question `10_1_3_Q12` [Extension] *(Telescoping Logarithm Sum)*
Evaluate the exact sum $\log_2\!\left(\frac{2}{1}\right) + \log_2\!\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) + \log_2\!\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) + \dots + \log_2\!\left(\frac{16}{15}\right)$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By the product law, the sum of the logarithms equals the logarithm of the product of their arguments: $\log_2\!\left(\frac{2}{1} \times \frac{3}{2} \times \frac{4}{3} \times \dots \times \frac{16}{15}\right)$.
2. All intermediate numerators and denominators cancel, leaving $\log_2\!\left(\frac{16}{1}\right) = \log_2(2^4) = 4$.

**Final Answer**: $4$
</details>
