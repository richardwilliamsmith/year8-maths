### Key Vocabulary
- **Compound interest**: Interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous compounding periods: $A = P(1 + r)^n$.
- **Compounding period**: The time interval between successive calculations and additions of interest (e.g. annually, quarterly, monthly, or daily).
- **Reducing-balance depreciation**: A model in which an asset loses a fixed percentage $r$ of its current book value each period: $A = P(1 - r)^n$.
- **Rounding error accumulation**: The discrepancy that grows when intermediate values or interest rates are rounded prematurely before being raised to a power in repeated financial calculations.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### The Compound Interest Formula

Under simple interest, interest is calculated only on the original principal $P$, producing linear growth. Under compound interest, the interest earned at the end of each period is added to the principal, so that future interest is earned on a growing balance. If a principal $P$ is invested at an interest rate $r$ (expressed as a decimal or exact fraction per compounding period) for $n$ compounding periods, the total amount $A$ is given by an exponential growth model, and the compound interest earned is $I = A - P$.

$$A = P(1 + r)^n, \quad I = A - P$$

> **💡 Convert Percentage Rates to Decimal or Fraction First**
> If the interest rate is $6\%$ per annum compounded annually, write $r = 0.06$ (or $\frac{6}{100}$) so that the growth multiplier for each period is $1 + r = 1.06$.

### Non-Annual Compounding Periods and Exact Representations

When interest is Quoted at a nominal annual rate $R\%$ per annum (p.a.) but compounded $k$ times per year (such as $k = 2$ half-yearly, $k = 4$ quarterly, or $k = 12$ monthly) for $t$ years, you must convert both the rate and the time to match the compounding period before substituting into the formula. The rate per period is $r = \frac{R}{100k}$ and the total number of compounding periods is $n = k \times t$. When $\frac{R}{100k}$ is a recurring decimal (such as $8\%$ p.a. compounded monthly), always retain the exact fraction in your calculation until the final rounding to the nearest cent.

$$A = P\left(1 + \frac{R}{100k}\right)^{kt}$$

> **💡 Match Rate and Periods, and Never Round Early**
> Divide the annual rate by $k$ and multiply the number of years by $k$. Keep exact fractions like $\frac{0.08}{12} = \frac{1}{150}$ inside the brackets; rounding intermediate decimals before raising to power $n$ causes significant rounding error accumulation.

### Reducing-Balance Depreciation and Inflation

Equipment, vehicles, and technology lose value over time. In reducing-balance depreciation, an asset of initial value $P$ decreases by $r$ ($R\%$) of its current book value each year, so its depreciated value $A$ after $n$ years decays exponentially. Conversely, inflation models the rising cost of goods over time using the compound growth formula $A = P(1 + i)^n$, where $i$ is the annual inflation rate.

$$A = P(1 - r)^n = P\left(1 - \frac{R}{100}\right)^n$$

> **💡 Growth Uses $(1 + r)$, Decay Uses $(1 - r)$**
> A car depreciating at $15\%$ per year retains $85\%$ of its value each year, so its multiplier is $1 - 0.15 = 0.85$. Total depreciation after $n$ years is $P - A$.

## Worked Examples

### WE10_1_1_1: Calculating Annual Compound Interest

**Problem**: Find the final balance and the total compound interest earned if $\$5000$ is invested at $6\%$ p.a. compounded annually for $3$ years.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Identify the principal $P = 5000$, annual interest rate $r = 0.06$, and number of compounding periods $n = 3$. | $$P = 5000, \quad r = 0.06, \quad n = 3$$ |
| 2 | Substitute into the compound interest formula $A = P(1 + r)^n$ and evaluate. | $$A = 5000(1 + 0.06)^3 = 5000(1.06)^3 = \$5955.08$$ |
| 3 | Subtract the initial principal $P$ from the final amount $A$ to find the compound interest $I$. | $$I = A - P = 5955.08 - 5000 = \$955.08$$ |

**Answer**: Final balance $\$5955.08$; compound interest $\$955.08$

> **✏️ Now You Try**

> **Working**: $A = 10\,000(1.05)^3 = \$11\,576.25$; $I = 11\,576.25 - 10\,000 = \$1576.25$.
> **Answer**: Final balance $\$11\,576.25$; compound interest $\$1576.25$

### WE10_1_1_2: Compounding Quarterly vs Annually

**Problem**: An amount of $\$8000$ is invested at $8\%$ p.a. compounded quarterly for $1$ year. Find the final value of the investment and compare it with $\$8000$ invested at $8\%$ p.a. compounded annually for $1$ year.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | For quarterly compounding ($k = 4$), divide the annual rate by $4$ and multiply the number of years by $4$. | $$r = \frac{8\%}{4} = 2\% = 0.02, \quad n = 1 \times 4 = 4\text{ quarters}$$ |
| 2 | Calculate the final amount for quarterly compounding. | $$A_{\text{quarterly}} = 8000(1.02)^4 = \$8659.45728 \approx \$8659.46$$ |
| 3 | Compare with annual compounding ($A_{\text{annual}} = 8000(1.08)^1 = \$8640$). | $$\text{Extra interest} = 8659.46 - 8640.00 = \$19.46$$ |

**Answer**: $\$8659.46$ (which is $\$19.46$ more than annual compounding)

> **Working**: $A = 20\,000(1.03)^4 = \$22\,510.18$.

### WE10_1_1_3: Modelling Reducing-Balance Depreciation

**Problem**: A Melbourne printing business buys a commercial printer for $\$25\,000$. It depreciates on a reducing-balance basis at $20\%$ p.a. Find: (a) its book value after $3$ years, and (b) the total amount by which it has depreciated.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | Identify $P = 25\,000$, depreciation rate $r = 0.20$, and $n = 3$ years. The decay multiplier is $1 - 0.20 = 0.80$. | $$A = 25\,000(1 - 0.20)^3 = 25\,000(0.8)^3$$ |
| 2 | Evaluate $0.8^3 = 0.512$ and multiply by $25\,000$ to find the book value after $3$ years. | $$A = 25\,000 \times 0.512 = \$12\,800$$ |
| 3 | Subtract the depreciated book value from the original purchase price to find the total depreciation. | $$\text{Total depreciation} = 25\,000 - 12\,800 = \$12\,200$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\$12\,800$, (b) $\$12\,200$

> **✏️ Now You Try**

> *Hint*: Use $A = 40\,000(1 - 0.15)^2 = 40\,000(0.85)^2$.
> **Working**: $A = 40\,000 \times 0.7225 = \$28\,900$.
> **Answer**: $\$28\,900$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Forgetting to Adjust Both the Rate and Number of Periods for Non-Annual Compounding
- **The Misconception**: Using the annual rate $r = 0.06$ with $n = 24$ months, or dividing the rate by $12$ while leaving $n = 2$ years.
- **The Correct Concept**: In $A = P(1 + r)^n$, $r$ and $n$ must be expressed in the same time unit (per compounding period). For $6\%$ p.a. compounded monthly for $2$ years, $r = \frac{0.06}{12} = 0.005$ per month AND $n = 2 \times 12 = 24$ months.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$A = 1000(1.06)^{24} \quad \text{or} \quad A = 1000(1.005)^2 \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$A = 1000\left(1 + \frac{0.06}{12}\right)^{2 \times 12} = 1000(1.005)^{24} \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: Using $1000(1.06)^{24}$ treats $6\%$ as a monthly rate ($72\%$ p.a.), inflating the balance to $\$4048.93$ instead of the true $\$1127.16$.

### Prematurely Rounding the Periodic Interest Rate
- **The Misconception**: Rounding a recurring periodic rate such as $\frac{8\%}{12} = 0.00666\ldots$ to $0.007$ before raising $(1 + r)$ to a large power $n$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Small rounding errors in the base $(1 + r)$ are magnified exponentially when raised to a large power $n$. Always enter the exact fraction $1 + \frac{0.08}{12}$ into your calculator and round only the final dollar amount.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$A = 100\,000(1.007)^{60} = \$151\,969.29 \quad (\text{Incorrect: rounded rate early})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$A = 100\,000\left(1 + \frac{0.08}{12}\right)^{60} = \$148\,984.57 \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: Over $60$ months on $\$100\,000$, rounding $0.00666\ldots$ to $0.007$ creates an error of $\$2984.72$!

#### Question `10_1_1_Q6` [Medium] *(Inflation Modelling)*

#### Question `10_1_1_Q7` [Challenging] *(Present Value Calculation)*
Priya wants to have $\$12\,100$ in her savings account in $2$ years' time to buy a used car. How much principal $P$ must she deposit today into an account paying $10\%$ p.a. compounded annually?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Substitute $A = 12\,100$, $r = 0.10$, and $n = 2$ into $A = P(1 + r)^2$: $12\,100 = P(1.10)^2 = 1.21P$.
2. Divide both sides by $1.21$: $P = \frac{12\,100}{1.21} = \$10\,000$.

**Final Answer**: $\$10\,000$
</details>

#### Question `10_1_1_Q8` [Challenging] *(Comparing Simple and Compound Interest)*
Liam compares two investment options for $\$5000$ over $2$ years: Option A pays $6.5\%$ p.a. simple interest, while Option B pays $6\%$ p.a. compounded annually. Which option yields the greater final balance, and by how much?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. For Option A (simple interest): $A_A = 5000(1 + 0.065 \times 2) = 5000(1.13) = \$5650$.
2. For Option B (compound interest): $A_B = 5000(1.06)^2 = 5000(1.1236) = \$5618$.
3. Difference: $5650 - 5618 = \$32$ in favour of Option A.

**Final Answer**: Option A yields $\$5650$ and Option B yields $\$5618$; Option A is greater by $\$32$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `10_1_1_Q9` [Medium] *(Effect of Rounding Approximations)*
A student calculates the balance of $\$150\,000$ invested at $8\%$ p.a. compounded monthly for $1$ month by rounding $\frac{0.08}{12} = 0.00666\ldots$ to $0.0067$. Find the exact balance after $1$ month and determine the dollar error caused by this rounding.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Using the exact fraction $r = \frac{0.08}{12} = \frac{1}{150}$, $A_{\text{exact}} = 150\,000\left(1 + \frac{1}{150}\right) = 150\,000 + 1000 = \$151\,000$.
2. Using the rounded rate $r \approx 0.0067$, $A_{\text{approx}} = 150\,000(1.0067) = \$151\,005$.
3. The premature rounding introduces an error of $151\,005 - 151\,000 = \$5$ in just one month.

**Final Answer**: Exact balance $\$151\,000$; rounded calculation gives $\$151\,005$ (an error of $\$5$)
</details>

#### Question `10_1_1_Q10` [Medium] *(Exponential vs Linear Depreciation)*
Explain why an asset depreciating by $20\%$ p.a. under the reducing-balance method does NOT lose $100\%$ of its value after $5$ years, and state the exact fraction of its original value that remains after $5$ years.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Under reducing-balance depreciation, the $20\%$ deduction each year is taken from the current book value, not the initial price $P$.
2. After $5$ years, the remaining fraction of value is $(1 - 0.20)^5 = \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^5 = \frac{1024}{3125} = 0.32768$ ($32.768\%$), which is strictly positive.

**Final Answer**: Each $20\%$ reduction applies to the reduced balance rather than the original price; $\left(\frac{4}{5}\right)^5 = \frac{1024}{3125}$ ($32.768\%$) remains
</details>

#### Question `10_1_1_Q11` [Challenging] *(Effective Annual Interest Rate)*
Find the effective annual interest rate (as an exact percentage) of a loan charging $10\%$ p.a. compounded half-yearly, and explain why it is greater than $10\%$ p.a.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Over $1$ year ($2$ half-years at $5\%$ per half-year), the growth factor is $(1 + 0.05)^2 = 1.1025$.
2. Subtracting $1$ gives the effective annual rate: $1.1025 - 1 = 0.1025 = 10.25\%$, where the extra $0.25\%$ is $5\%$ interest earned on the first half-year's $5\%$ interest.

**Final Answer**: $10.25\%$ p.a., because interest earned in the first half-year earns additional interest in the second half-year
</details>

#### Question `10_1_1_Q12` [Extension] *(Algebraic Proof of Compound vs Simple Interest)*
Prove algebraically that if a principal $P$ is invested at rate $r$ per period for $2$ periods, the difference between the compound interest earned and the simple interest earned is $Pr^2$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. For $2$ periods, the compound amount is $A_C = P(1 + r)^2 = P(1 + 2r + r^2) = P + 2Pr + Pr^2$, so $I_C = 2Pr + Pr^2$.
2. The simple interest for $2$ periods is $I_S = P \times r \times 2 = 2Pr$.
3. Subtracting gives $I_C - I_S = (2Pr + Pr^2) - 2Pr = Pr^2$, which is precisely the interest earned in period $2$ on period $1$'s interest $Pr$.

**Final Answer**: $I_{\text{compound}} - I_{\text{simple}} = P[(1 + r)^2 - 1] - 2Pr = Pr^2$
</details>
