### Key Vocabulary
- **Terminating decimal**: A decimal expansion that ends after a finite number of digits after the decimal point (e.g., $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$).
- **Recurring (repeating) decimal**: A decimal expansion in which a digit or block of digits repeats infinitely in a regular cycle (e.g., $\frac{2}{11} = 0.181818\dots = 0.\overline{18}$).
- **Vinculum / Dot notation**: Notation used to mark the repeating cycle of a recurring decimal: a bar over the repeating block ($0.\overline{142857}$) or dots over the first and last repeating digits ($0.\dot{1}4285\dot{7}$).

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Predicting Terminating vs Recurring Decimals

Because our decimal system is based on powers of $10$, and the prime factorisation of $10$ is $2 \times 5$, a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ written in simplest form produces a terminating decimal if and only if the prime factorisation of the denominator $b$ contains only the primes $2$ and/or $5$. If the simplified denominator has any other prime factor (such as $3, 7, 11, 13$), its decimal expansion is guaranteed to be a recurring decimal.

$$\frac{a}{b} \text{ (in simplest form) terminates} \iff b = 2^m \times 5^n \text{ for non-negative integers } m, n$$

> **💡 Simplify Before Testing the Denominator**
> Always simplify the fraction first! For example, $\frac{6}{15}$ looks like it has a factor of $3$ in the denominator, but it simplifies to $\frac{2}{5} = 0.4$, which terminates.

### Converting Fractions to Terminating or Recurring Decimals

To convert any fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ to a decimal, either scale the denominator to a power of $10$ ($10, 100, 1000, \dots$) or divide the numerator $a$ by the denominator $b$ using short division ($a \div b$). Once a non-zero remainder repeats during short division, the quotient digits will repeat in the exact same cycle forever.

$$\frac{5}{12} = 5 \div 12 = 0.41666\dots = 0.41\dot{6}$$

> **💡 Dot vs Bar (Vinculum) Notation**
> For a single repeating digit, place one dot over that digit ($0.41\dot{6}$). For a block of two or more repeating digits, place a bar over the entire repeating block ($0.\overline{27}$) or dots on the first and last digits of the repeating block ($0.\dot{2}\dot{7}$).

### Converting Terminating and Simple Recurring Decimals to Fractions

A terminating decimal with $k$ decimal places can be written directly over $10^k$ and simplified. Furthermore, since $\frac{1}{9} = 0.\dot{1}$, $\frac{1}{99} = 0.\overline{01}$, and $\frac{1}{999} = 0.\overline{001}$, any pure recurring decimal with a $k$-digit repeating block starting immediately after the decimal point equals that $k$-digit integer divided by $\underbrace{99\dots 9}_{k\text{ nines}}$.

$$0.\dot{a} = \frac{a}{9}, \quad 0.\overline{ab} = \frac{ab}{99}, \quad 0.\overline{abc} = \frac{abc}{999}$$

> **💡 The Nines Denominator Pattern**
> Count how many digits are in the repeating cycle immediately after the decimal point and place the repeating block over that many $9$s, then simplify (e.g., $0.\overline{45} = \frac{45}{99} = \frac{5}{11}$).

## Worked Examples

### WE2_2_1: Classifying Fractions as Terminating or Recurring

**Problem**: Without performing division, determine whether each fraction produces a terminating or recurring decimal: (a) $\dfrac{13}{40}$ and (b) $\dfrac{21}{28}$ and (c) $\dfrac{7}{18}$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), $\frac{13}{40}$ is already in simplest form. Factorise the denominator $40 = 2^3 \times 5^1$. Since the only prime factors are $2$ and $5$, it terminates. | $$40 = 2^3 \times 5 \implies \frac{13}{40} = \frac{13 \times 25}{1000} = 0.325 \text{ (Terminating)}$$ |
| 2 | In part (b), simplify $\frac{21}{28}$ first by dividing by $7$: $\frac{21}{28} = \frac{3}{4}$. Since $4 = 2^2$, the decimal terminates. | $$\frac{21}{28} = \frac{3}{4} = 0.75 \text{ (Terminating)}$$ |
| 3 | In part (c), $\frac{7}{18}$ is in simplest form and $18 = 2 \times 3^2$. Because of the prime factor $3^2$, the decimal is recurring. | $$18 = 2 \times 3^2 \implies \frac{7}{18} = 0.3\dot{8} \text{ (Recurring)}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Terminating ($0.325$), (b) Terminating ($0.75$), (c) Recurring ($0.3\dot{8}$)

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Determine whether (a) $\dfrac{9}{24}$ and (b) $\dfrac{11}{30}$ are terminating or recurring decimals, and find their decimal forms.
> *Hint*: Simplify $\frac{9}{24}$ by dividing numerator and denominator by $3$ first.
> **Working**: (a) $\frac{9}{24} = \frac{3}{8} = 0.375$ (Terminating); (b) $30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5$, so $\frac{11}{30} = 0.3\dot{6}$ (Recurring).
> **Answer**: (a) Terminating ($0.375$), (b) Recurring ($0.3\dot{6}$)

### WE2_2_2: Converting Fractions to Recurring Decimals by Division

**Problem**: Convert $\dfrac{5}{11}$ and $-\dfrac{7}{12}$ to recurring decimals using correct recurring notation.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | For $\frac{5}{11}$, divide $5.0000\dots$ by $11$ or scale the denominator to $99$ by multiplying top and bottom by $9$. | $$\frac{5}{11} = \frac{45}{99} = 0.454545\dots = 0.\overline{45} \text{ (or } 0.\dot{4}\dot{5}\text{)}$$ |
| 2 | For $-\frac{7}{12}$, divide $7.000\dots$ by $12$: $70 \div 12 = 5$ rem $10$; $100 \div 12 = 8$ rem $4$; $40 \div 12 = 3$ rem $4$. Only the digit $3$ repeats. | $$-\frac{7}{12} = -0.58333\dots = -0.58\dot{3}$$ |

**Answer**: $0.\overline{45}$ and $-0.58\dot{3}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Convert $\dfrac{8}{11}$ and $-\dfrac{5}{6}$ to recurring decimals.
> *Hint*: Write $\frac{8}{11} = \frac{72}{99}$, and divide $5 \div 6$ for the second fraction.
> **Working**: $\frac{8}{11} = \frac{72}{99} = 0.\overline{72}$; $-\frac{5}{6} = -0.8333\dots = -0.8\dot{3}$.
> **Answer**: $0.\overline{72}$ and $-0.8\dot{3}$

### WE2_2_3: Converting Pure Recurring Decimals to Simplest Fractions

**Problem**: Express (a) $0.\dot{7}$ and (b) $0.\overline{36}$ as fractions in simplest form.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | In part (a), a single digit ($7$) repeats immediately after the decimal point, so place $7$ over a single $9$. | $$0.\dot{7} = \frac{7}{9}$$ |
| 2 | In part (b), two digits ($36$) repeat immediately after the decimal point, so place $36$ over $99$ and simplify by dividing by $\text{HCF}(36, 99) = 9$. | $$0.\overline{36} = \frac{36}{99} = \frac{36 \div 9}{99 \div 9} = \frac{4}{11}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) $\dfrac{7}{9}$, (b) $\dfrac{4}{11}$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Express (a) $0.\dot{6}$ and (b) $0.\overline{81}$ as fractions in simplest form.
> *Hint*: Write over $9$ and $99$ respectively, then divide by the HCF.
> **Working**: (a) $0.\dot{6} = \frac{6}{9} = \frac{2}{3}$; (b) $0.\overline{81} = \frac{81}{99} = \frac{9}{11}$.
> **Answer**: (a) $\dfrac{2}{3}$, (b) $\dfrac{9}{11}$

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Placing the Repeating Bar Over Non-Repeating Digits
- **The Misconception**: Writing $0.58333\dots$ as $0.\overline{583}$ instead of $0.58\dot{3}$.
- **The Correct Concept**: The recurring dot or bar must cover ONLY the digits that actually repeat. In $0.58333\dots$, the digits $5$ and $8$ appear only once, while only $3$ repeats.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\frac{7}{12} = 0.58333\dots = 0.\overline{583} \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\frac{7}{12} = 0.58333\dots = 0.58\dot{3} \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: $0.\overline{583}$ means $0.583583583\dots = \frac{583}{999}$, which differs from $\frac{7}{12} = 0.583333\dots$ in the fourth decimal place.

### Treating a Rounded Calculator Display as an Exact Terminating Decimal
- **The Misconception**: Concluding that $\frac{2}{3} = 0.666666667$ is a terminating decimal because a 9-digit calculator rounds the final digit to $7$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Calculators have a finite screen width and round the last displayed digit. Because $3$ is a prime factor of the denominator, $\frac{2}{3} = 0.\dot{6}$ continues infinitely.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\frac{2}{3} = 0.666666667 = \frac{666666667}{10^9} \quad (\text{Rounding approximation only})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\frac{2}{3} = 0.666\dots = 0.\dot{6} \quad (\text{Exact recurring decimal})$$
- **Counter-Example**: Multiplying $0.666666667 \times 3$ gives $2.000000001 \neq 2$.

#### Question `2_2_Q6` [Challenging] *(Algebraic Conversion)*
Use algebra to convert $x = 0.2\dot{7}$ ($0.2777\dots$) into a fraction in simplest form.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Let $x = 0.2777\dots$. Then $10x = 2.777\dots$ and $100x = 27.777\dots$.
2. Subtract the two equations so the repeating tail cancels: $100x - 10x = 27.777\dots - 2.777\dots \implies 90x = 25$.
3. Solve for $x$ and simplify: $x = \frac{25}{90} = \frac{5}{18}$.

**Final Answer**: $\dfrac{5}{18}$
</details>

#### Question `2_2_Q8` [Medium] *(Australian Currency Rounding)*
Three friends in Hobart split a restaurant bill of $\$100$ equally. (a) Write each person's exact share as an improper fraction and as a recurring decimal. (b) If two friends pay $\$33.33$ and the third pays $\$33.34$, how much more does the third friend pay than the exact mathematical share?

#### Question `2_2_Q9` [Challenging] *(Cyclic Pattern Modelling)*
Convert the decimal $\dfrac{1}{7}$ to a recurring decimal and use the length of its repeating cycle to find the $50\text{th}$ digit after the decimal point.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. By division, $\frac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857}$, which has a repeating cycle of length $6$.
2. Divide $50$ by the cycle length $6$: $50 = 6 \times 8 + 2$ (remainder $2$).
3. The $50\text{th}$ digit is the $2\text{nd}$ digit in the cycle $142857$, which is $4$.

**Final Answer**: $\dfrac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857}$; the $50\text{th}$ digit is $4$
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `2_2_Q10` [Challenging] *(Pigeonhole Principle & Division)*
Explain why the recurring decimal expansion of any rational number $\dfrac{a}{b}$ (where $b > 1$) can have a repeating cycle of length at most $b - 1$ digits.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. When dividing by $b$ in long division, if the decimal does not terminate (remainder $0$), the only possible remainders at each step are $1, 2, 3, \dots, b - 1$.
2. Since there are only $b - 1$ distinct non-zero remainders, after at most $b - 1$ steps a remainder must repeat.
3. Once a remainder repeats, the subsequent division steps and quotient digits repeat identically, so the cycle length cannot exceed $b - 1$.

**Final Answer**: There are only $b - 1$ possible non-zero remainders ($1, 2, \dots, b - 1$), so a remainder must repeat within at most $b$ steps
</details>

#### Question `2_2_Q11` [Challenging] *(Mathematical Proof)*
Show using two different methods that $0.\dot{9} = 1$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Method 1 (Fractions): Since $\frac{1}{3} = 0.\dot{3}$, multiplying both sides by $3$ gives $1 = 3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 3 \times 0.\dot{3} = 0.\dot{9}$.
2. Method 2 (Algebra): Let $x = 0.999\dots$. Then $10x = 9.999\dots$. Subtracting gives $9x = 9$, so $x = 1$.

**Final Answer**: $0.\dot{9} = 3 \times 0.\dot{3} = 3 \times \frac{1}{3} = 1$
</details>

#### Question `2_2_Q12` [Extension] *(Prime Factorisation Classification)*
Find all integers $n$ between $1$ and $20$ inclusive for which $\dfrac{1}{n}$ is a terminating decimal. Justify your list using prime factorisation.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. $\frac{1}{n}$ terminates if and only if $n = 2^a \times 5^b$ for integers $a, b \ge 0$.
2. Powers of $2$ up to $20$: $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$.
3. Multiples of $5$ formed with powers of $2$ up to $20$: $5, 10, 20$.
4. Combining these gives the complete list: $1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 16, 20$.

**Final Answer**: $n \in \{1, 2, 4, 5, 8, 10, 16, 20\}$
</details>
