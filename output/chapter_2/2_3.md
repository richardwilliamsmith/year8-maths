### Key Vocabulary
- **Irrational number**: A real number that cannot be expressed as a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ of two integers; its decimal expansion is infinite and non-recurring (e.g., $\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{5}, \pi$).
- **Surd**: An irrational root of a rational number, such as $\sqrt{3}$ or $\sqrt[3]{10}$, left in exact radical form.
- **Real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$)**: The complete set of all rational numbers and all irrational numbers, corresponding to every point on the continuous number line.

## Theory & Key Ideas

### Rational vs Irrational Numbers and the Real Number System

Every real number ($\mathbb{R}$) is either rational ($\mathbb{Q}$) or irrational. A number is rational if and only if it can be written as a fraction $\frac{a}{b}$ (where $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}, b \neq 0$), which corresponds to a terminating or recurring decimal. Numbers whose decimal expansions continue forever without any repeating cycle cannot be written as fractions of integers and are called irrational numbers.

$$\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup \text{Irrationals}, \quad \mathbb{Q} \cap \text{Irrationals} = \varnothing$$

> **💡 Decimal Test for Rational vs Irrational**
> Terminates or repeats in a cycle $\implies$ Rational ($\frac{a}{b}$). Never terminates and never repeats $\implies$ Irrational (such as $\pi = 3.14159265\dots$ or $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356\dots$).

### Square Roots, Cube Roots, and Surds

If a positive integer $n$ is a perfect square ($1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \dots$), then $\sqrt{n}$ is an integer and therefore rational. If $n$ is not a perfect square, then $\sqrt{n}$ is an irrational number called a surd. Note that the radical symbol $\sqrt{n}$ always denotes the non-negative (principal) square root, while cube roots of negative numbers are real and negative: $\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2$.

$$\sqrt{a^2} = a \text{ for } a \ge 0, \quad \sqrt[3]{-a^3} = -a$$

> **💡 Not All Square Roots Are Irrational**
> Always evaluate the root first before classifying! $\sqrt{49} = 7$ and $\sqrt{\frac{9}{16}} = \frac{3}{4}$ are RATIONAL, whereas $\sqrt{50}$ is IRRATIONAL.

### Bounding and Estimating Surds on a Number Line

Because $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ increases as $x$ increases, you can locate any surd $\sqrt{n}$ between two consecutive integers by finding the largest perfect square $a^2 < n$ and the smallest perfect square $b^2 > n$.

$$a^2 < n < b^2 \implies a < \sqrt{n} < b \quad (a, b > 0)$$

> **💡 Sandwiching a Surd Between Consecutive Integers**
> To estimate $\sqrt{40}$, sandwich $40$ between the nearest perfect squares $36$ and $49$: since $36 < 40 < 49$, taking square roots gives $6 < \sqrt{40} < 7$ (and since $40$ is closer to $36$, $\sqrt{40} \approx 6.3$).

### The Irrational Number $\pi$ vs Rational Approximations

The number $\pi$ (pi) is defined as the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter: $\pi = \frac{C}{d}$. In the 18th century, Johann Lambert proved that $\pi$ is irrational. Common values such as $3.14$ or $\frac{22}{7} = 3.\overline{142857}$ are convenient rational approximations, not the exact value of $\pi$.

$$\pi = 3.14159265358979\dots \neq \frac{22}{7} = 3.142857142857\dots$$

> **💡 Exact Value vs Rounded Value**
> When a question asks for an 'exact answer', leave $\pi$ or the surd symbol (e.g., $12\pi\text{ cm}$ or $\sqrt{29}\text{ m}$) in your final answer rather than rounding a decimal.

## Worked Examples

### WE2_3_1: Classifying Real Numbers as Rational or Irrational

**Problem**: Classify each of the following numbers as rational or irrational: (a) $\sqrt{81}$, (b) $\sqrt{20}$, (c) $-0.\overline{42}$, and (d) $2\pi$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | For (a), $81 = 9^2$ is a perfect square, so $\sqrt{81} = 9 = \frac{9}{1}$, which is a ratio of integers. | $$\sqrt{81} = 9 \implies \text{Rational}$$ |
| 2 | For (b), $20$ lies strictly between the consecutive perfect squares $16$ and $25$, so $\sqrt{20}$ is a surd. | $$16 < 20 < 25 \implies \sqrt{20} \text{ is Irrational}$$ |
| 3 | For (c), $-0.\overline{42} = -\frac{42}{99} = -\frac{14}{33}$ is a recurring decimal, so it is rational. For (d), multiplying the irrational number $\pi$ by $2$ produces a non-terminating, non-recurring decimal. | $$-0.\overline{42} = -\frac{14}{33} \text{ (Rational)}, \quad 2\pi \text{ (Irrational)}$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Rational, (b) Irrational, (c) Rational, (d) Irrational

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Classify each of the following numbers as rational or irrational: (a) $\sqrt{30}$, (b) $\sqrt{\dfrac{25}{64}}$, (c) $\dfrac{22}{7}$, and (d) $\sqrt[3]{-27}$.
> *Hint*: Remember that $\frac{22}{7}$ is already written as a fraction of two integers.
> **Working**: (a) $30$ is not a square $\implies$ Irrational; (b) $\sqrt{25/64} = 5/8 \implies$ Rational; (c) $22/7 \implies$ Rational; (d) $\sqrt[3]{-27} = -3 \implies$ Rational.
> **Answer**: (a) Irrational, (b) Rational, (c) Rational, (d) Rational

### WE2_3_2: Bounding a Surd Between Consecutive Integers

**Problem**: Without using a calculator, find the two consecutive integers between which (a) $\sqrt{75}$ and (b) $-\sqrt{42}$ lie.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | For part (a), identify the perfect squares immediately below and above $75$: $8^2 = 64$ and $9^2 = 81$. | $$64 < 75 < 81 \implies \sqrt{64} < \sqrt{75} < \sqrt{81} \implies 8 < \sqrt{75} < 9$$ |
| 2 | For part (b), first bound $\sqrt{42}$ between $\sqrt{36} = 6$ and $\sqrt{49} = 7$, then negate the inequality (reversing the order on the negative number line). | $$36 < 42 < 49 \implies 6 < \sqrt{42} < 7 \implies -7 < -\sqrt{42} < -6$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Between $8$ and $9$; (b) Between $-7$ and $-6$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: Without using a calculator, find the two consecutive integers between which (a) $\sqrt{110}$ and (b) $-\sqrt{55}$ lie.
> *Hint*: Compare $110$ with $10^2 = 100$ and $11^2 = 121$; compare $55$ with $7^2 = 49$ and $8^2 = 64$.
> **Working**: (a) $100 < 110 < 121 \implies 10 < \sqrt{110} < 11$; (b) $49 < 55 < 64 \implies -8 < -\sqrt{55} < -7$.
> **Answer**: (a) Between $10$ and $11$; (b) Between $-8$ and $-7$

### WE2_3_3: Exact vs Approximated Values in Applied Geometry

**Problem**: A square solar panel on a rooftop in Mildura has an area of $3\text{ m}^2$, and a circular water tank lid has a diameter of $2\text{ m}$. (a) State the exact side length of the solar panel and the exact circumference of the lid ($C = \pi d$). (b) Calculate the positive difference between $\dfrac{22}{7}$ and $\pi$ correct to $4$ decimal places, given $\pi \approx 3.14159$.

| Step | Thinking | Working |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | The side length of a square of area $A = 3\text{ m}^2$ is $\sqrt{3}\text{ m}$ (an irrational surd). The circumference of a circle with $d = 2\text{ m}$ is $C = 2\pi\text{ m}$. | $$s = \sqrt{3}\text{ m}, \quad C = 2\pi\text{ m}$$ |
| 2 | Evaluate $\frac{22}{7} \approx 3.14286$ and subtract $\pi \approx 3.14159$. | $$\frac{22}{7} - \pi \approx 3.142857 - 3.141593 = 0.001264 \approx 0.0013$$ |

**Answer**: (a) Side length $= \sqrt{3}\text{ m}$, circumference $= 2\pi\text{ m}$; (b) $0.0013$

> **✏️ Now You Try**
> **Problem**: A square courtyard in Adelaide has an area of $18\text{ m}^2$. State its exact perimeter in surd form, and determine which two consecutive integers the perimeter lies between.
> *Hint*: One side has length $\sqrt{18}\text{ m}$, so the perimeter is $4\sqrt{18} = \sqrt{16 \times 18} = \sqrt{288}\text{ m}$.
> **Working**: Exact perimeter $= 4\sqrt{18}\text{ m}$. Since $16^2 = 256 < 288 < 17^2 = 289$, the perimeter lies between $16\text{ m}$ and $17\text{ m}$.
> **Answer**: Exact perimeter $= 4\sqrt{18}\text{ m}$ (between $16\text{ m}$ and $17\text{ m}$)

## ⚠️ Common Misconceptions Alert

### Assuming $\pi$ Equals $\frac{22}{7}$ or $3.14$ Exactly
- **The Misconception**: Classifying $\pi$ as a rational number because students have used $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$ in primary school.
- **The Correct Concept**: $\frac{22}{7} = 3.\overline{142857}$ is a rational number that approximates $\pi$ to $2$ decimal places, whereas $\pi = 3.14159265\dots$ is strictly irrational.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\pi = \frac{22}{7} \in \mathbb{Q} \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\pi \approx \frac{22}{7}, \quad \text{where } \pi \text{ is irrational and } \frac{22}{7} \text{ is rational}$$
- **Counter-Example**: At the third decimal place, $\frac{22}{7} = 3.1428\dots$ while $\pi = 3.1415\dots$, so $\frac{22}{7} > \pi$.

### Adding Under Square Roots: $\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} \neq \sqrt{a + b}$
- **The Misconception**: Simplifying $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$ as $\sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.
- **The Correct Concept**: Square roots do NOT distribute over addition or subtraction. You must evaluate each square root separately before adding: $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7$.
- **❌ Incorrect Working**: $$\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \quad (\text{Incorrect})$$
- **✅ Correct Working**: $$\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 = 7 \quad (\text{Correct})$$
- **Counter-Example**: $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 7$, which is strictly greater than $\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$.

#### Question `2_3_Q6` [Challenging] *(Ordering Real Numbers)*
Arrange the following real numbers in ascending order without a calculator: $\pi$, $\sqrt{10}$, $3.14$, and $\dfrac{22}{7}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

### Problem-Solving & Modelling

#### Question `2_3_Q7` [Medium] *(Victorian Farming (Gippsland))*
A square paddock in Gippsland has an area of $150\text{ m}^2$. Farmers want to fence all $4$ sides using wire sold only in whole-metre rolls. What is the minimum whole number of metres of wire they must buy to enclose the entire perimeter?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. The side length of the square paddock is $s = \sqrt{150}\text{ m}$, so the total perimeter is $P = 4\sqrt{150} = \sqrt{16 \times 150} = \sqrt{2400}\text{ m}$.
2. Compare $2400$ with nearby squares: $48^2 = 2304$ and $49^2 = 2401$.
3. Since $48 < \sqrt{2400} < 49$, they must round up to $49\text{ m}$ of wire to have enough.

**Final Answer**: $49\text{ m}$
</details>

#### Question `2_3_Q8` [Medium] *(Sydney to Hobart Yacht Race)*
According to marine physics, the maximum hull speed $v$ (in knots) of a displacement yacht in the Sydney to Hobart Yacht Race is modelled by $v = \dfrac{4}{3}\sqrt{L}$, where $L$ is the waterline length in feet. Calculate the exact hull speed when $L = 36\text{ feet}$.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Substitute $L = 36$ into the formula: $v = \frac{4}{3}\sqrt{36}$.
2. Evaluate $\sqrt{36} = 6$: $v = \frac{4}{3} \times 6 = 8\text{ knots}$.

**Final Answer**: $8\text{ knots}$
</details>

#### Question `2_3_Q9` [Challenging] *(Inequalities with Surds)*
How many integers $n$ satisfy the inequality $\sqrt{20} < n < \sqrt{130}$?

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Since $4^2 = 16 < 20 < 25 = 5^2$, we have $4 < \sqrt{20} < 5$, so the smallest integer $n > \sqrt{20}$ is $5$.
2. Since $11^2 = 121 < 130 < 144 = 12^2$, we have $11 < \sqrt{130} < 12$, so the largest integer $n < \sqrt{130}$ is $11$.
3. The integers from $5$ to $11$ inclusive are $5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11$, which is $11 - 5 + 1 = 7$ integers.

**Final Answer**: $7\text{ integers}$ ($5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11$)
</details>

### Reasoning & Communication

#### Question `2_3_Q10` [Medium] *(Counter-Example Proof)*
Disprove the statement: 'The sum of two irrational numbers is always irrational' by providing a counter-example.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Let $a = \sqrt{2}$ and $b = -\sqrt{2}$ (or $b = 5 - \sqrt{2}$). Both $a$ and $b$ are irrational numbers.
2. Their sum is $a + b = \sqrt{2} + (-\sqrt{2}) = 0$ (or $\sqrt{2} + (5 - \sqrt{2}) = 5$), which is an integer and therefore rational.
3. Hence the statement is false.

**Final Answer**: Counter-example: $\sqrt{2} + (-\sqrt{2}) = 0$, which is rational
</details>

#### Question `2_3_Q11` [Challenging] *(Counter-Example Proof)*
Disprove the statement: 'The product of two irrational numbers is always irrational' by giving two examples: one using equal surds and one using two different surds.

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. Using equal surds: $\sqrt{3}$ is irrational, and $\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3} = 3$, which is rational.
2. Using distinct surds: $\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{8}$ are both irrational, and their product is $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4$, which is rational.

**Final Answer**: $\sqrt{3} \times \sqrt{3} = 3$ and $\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{16} = 4$
</details>

#### Question `2_3_Q12` [Extension] *(Irrationality of sqrt(2))*
Explain using prime factorisation why the square of any integer has an even number of prime factors (counted with multiplicity), and use this to explain why $2b^2 = a^2$ has no solution for positive integers $a$ and $b$ (proving $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational).

<details>
<summary><strong>Full Step-by-Step Solution</strong></summary>

1. When any positive integer is squared, every exponent in its prime factorisation is doubled, so the prime $2$ appears an even number of times in $a^2$ and an even number of times in $b^2$.
2. Multiplying $b^2$ by one extra factor of $2$ means $2b^2$ contains the prime factor $2$ an ODD number of times.
3. By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, a number with an odd number of factors of $2$ ($2b^2$) can never equal a number with an even number of factors of $2$ ($a^2$). Thus $\sqrt{2} \neq \frac{a}{b}$.

**Final Answer**: $a^2$ has an even power of $2$, whereas $2b^2$ has an odd power of $2$, contradicting unique prime factorisation
</details>
